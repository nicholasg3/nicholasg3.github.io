Decide the Rules Before Buying Tools

A list of AI tools is easy to sell as an AI strategy. It is also the first part to go out of date.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

Before a founder picks AI tools, they need a short set of operating beliefs about where AI may change the business and where human judgment stays in charge. Scarce attention this quarter should follow those beliefs.

Starting with tools feels practical because it starts with demos. A model drafts a support reply or summarizes a sales call, and each demo is plausible on its own. Stack enough of them and they look like a strategy, but what the company has is a backlog: more automations and still no account of where its advantage comes from.

Research in information systems and AI strategy offers a frame that lasts longer. Technology pays off when it lowers the cost of coordination or improves the quality of a specific decision, so strategy work should begin with four questions:

  1. Which decision gets noticeably better if the company can retrieve, synthesize or generate knowledge faster?
  2. Which workflow repeats often enough to justify automation, yet varies enough that naive automation will fail?
  3. What proprietary context makes the output better than a generic model's answer?
  4. Which human judgment has to stay explicit because a confident error would be expensive?
Be precise about caution: name the decision AI touches and the person who carries the risk if it goes wrong.

The answers turn AI spending into ordinary resource allocation, so a founder can rank candidate investments instead of treating every experiment as equal.

Customer support is a good candidate when the same questions keep coming in and existing policy already provides a cheap escalation path. Sales research makes sense when the bottleneck is understanding the account, and much less when it is drafting the message. Product discovery is a weaker case if customer context is thin, because the team will mostly generate more speculative requirements. Finance and compliance usually call for assistive workflows that stop short of autonomous execution, since a confident error there costs more than a slow human review.

Writing the rules down on one page

The company should state what it believes about AI before a vendor pitch or an investor's question forces an answer. At an early-stage company the first version fits on a page:

  • Use AI to speed up search and drafting where there is plenty of context.
  • Leave the final call to a person where trust, legal exposure or capital is at stake.
  • Tie each investment to a named business decision.
  • Expect output to improve as the company adds proprietary data or builds a regular operating cadence.
  • Treat adoption as a way for the team and the system to learn, beyond making a task cheaper.

The page will need revising. What it gives a founder, and a tool list cannot, is a clear basis for turning a proposal down.

The advantage goes to companies that know which decisions compound and which workflows improve the system, and that keep human responsibilities written down where a fluent interface invites trust without checking. A long automation backlog is no substitute.
