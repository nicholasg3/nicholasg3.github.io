Treat Each ASEAN Country as a Separate Market Test

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) is writing better digital rules, but Southeast Asia is still many markets. A founder needs narrower answers: which country to enter first, which operating pieces carry over to the next one, and which regional ambitions are still only talk.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

Regional stories appeal because they fit a complicated place onto one slide. Southeast Asia has young consumers, rising digital use, mobile payments and a great deal of official talk about integration, and "Association of Southeast Asian Nations" sounds tidy as a single label. Actual go-to-market plans still give each country a separate job: Singapore for trust, Indonesia for size, Vietnam for skills, Thailand for payments, Malaysia for a base, and the Philippines for English-language services.

Part of the regional account is real. ASEAN is building a more serious digital trade architecture. The Digital Economy Framework Agreement (DEFA), cross-border data rules, interoperable payments, e-commerce commitments, digital ID work and the broader Economic Community agenda all reduce friction for digital business in a region that remains fragmented.

A founder, though, enters a particular country, with its own buyer workflow, payment stack and support burden. Policy is converging faster than the commercial conditions a company actually meets from one country to the next.

The ASEAN opportunity is a sequence of adjacent country tests, each sharing some operating pieces with the one before.

How far digital integration has got

ASEAN leaders launched the Digital Economy Framework Agreement process in 2023 to negotiate a region-wide digital economy agreement. The official negotiating frame covers cross-border e-commerce, digital trade, digital payments, e-invoicing, digital ID, data flows, data protection, cybersecurity, online safety, competition, talent mobility, and cooperation on emerging topics. Indonesia's economic ministry reported substantial conclusion of DEFA negotiations in October 2025, and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) 2026 digital trade review treats the agreement as one part of a wider regional architecture for reducing digital trade costs.

The OECD figures are more useful than the pitch. According to the review, ASEAN digital trade exports reached USD 387 billion in 2022, nearly one fifth of total ASEAN exports and 6% of global digital trade, and digitally deliverable services have grown faster than both non-digitally deliverable services and goods exports. Whatever the consumer-app narrative says, the region already exports a meaningful amount of digital trade.

For a founder, this means a small company can become international earlier. AI workflow tools, compliance templates and developer tools can sell across borders before there is a local office. Payments firms, business software vendors and data products can find customers whose commercial lives are already regional even while regulation stays national.

The question that follows is whether any of this should change the order in which a small company tests countries, customers and operating systems.

Choose the first country by what the product must prove

The usual expansion slide starts with population and gross domestic product (GDP), then adds a line about mobile penetration: Indonesia is large, Singapore is rich, Vietnam is growing, Thailand has payments, Malaysia is balanced, the Philippines has services. All of it is roughly true, and none of it tells you where to go first.

A better starting point is the burden the product has to carry. A B2B AI governance product needs trust, a buyer who owns compliance, English-language access to executives and some tolerance for procurement. Creator monetization tools depend on payment conversion, simple tax treatment, social distribution and low support costs. For trade finance, the requirements are regulatory clarity, partnerships with banks or payment service providers, careful know-your-customer checks and a way to handle disputes. A publication that trains founders needs readers with budget, urgency and enough cross-border ambition to pay for strategy over local gossip.

Once that burden is named, the right first country is the one that will tell you most about it, which is often not the biggest.

Test Useful Signal False Positive
Buyer clarity A specific buyer has budget, urgency, and a repeatable reason to buy across borders. The market is large, but the actual first buyer is undefined.
Operating reuse Payments, invoicing, support, legal terms, data handling, and onboarding can be reused in the next country. The first launch works only because of founder relationships and manual exceptions.
Trust bridge Local partners, credentials, references, or institutions reduce the trust gap for a new entrant. The product has global credibility but no local purchase confidence.
Regulatory drag The compliance burden is bounded enough for a small company to operate professionally. The legal question is postponed until after customers arrive.
Regional option Success in the first market teaches something reusable about the second and third markets. The founder wins a country but learns nothing about regional scale.

A country is worth entering when it answers a hard question about the business; one that mainly enlarges the total-addressable-market slide is a weaker bet.

What Singapore can and cannot prove

Outside founders often default to Singapore as their ASEAN starting point, for good reasons: rule of law, English, capital, regional headquarters, sophisticated buyers, strong payments and a reputation for being easy for global companies to deal with. For a strategy publication, a fintech product, an AI governance tool or a B2B service, Singapore can be the bridge of trust into the rest of the region.

It can also hide the real test. A product may sell in Singapore because the customers are globally fluent, heavily banked, enterprise-facing and used to imported software. Success with them says nothing about Indonesia's mass market, Vietnam's operating networks, Thailand's consumer payments environment or the Philippines' service economy. Singapore can confirm credibility and price while leaving localization, distribution and support unproven.

So founders should ask what they are using Singapore to learn. "Will regional executives trust the product?" is a question Singapore can answer. "Does Southeast Asia want this?" is too broad for any single country to answer.

What Indonesia demands beyond its size

Indonesia is the opposite temptation. Its scale is real, and for many consumer, commerce, fintech and small and medium business products, ignoring it would be unserious. Size by itself is not a go-to-market plan, though, because it raises the prize and the variance at the same time.

Before entering Indonesia a founder needs to know whether the product benefits from local network effects, whether distribution requires partnerships, whether payments and identity flows fit the use case, whether support can cope with the language and the geography, and whether regulatory classification changes the economics. If the company needs mass-market proof or local depth, Indonesia can be the right first bet. If it needs a fast, low-noise read on willingness to pay, it is probably the wrong one.

I would treat Indonesia as a test of operational seriousness before thinking of it as the big market. Founders who know exactly which local constraint they are solving tend to do well there; those who arrive with a regional story and no answer for how the business will run inside the country tend to get punished.

Payments and data decide what carries over

Payments and data flows are the practical centre of the ASEAN digital story. DEFA, e-commerce commitments, interoperable QR work, digital ID efforts, model contractual clauses and data-management frameworks all try to reduce the hidden costs of cross-border digital work. They determine whether a founder can sell, invoice, support and renew without rebuilding the company in every jurisdiction.

The question here is the same one raised in The Stablecoin Distribution Test: which payment and data stack reduces friction for this specific buyer while preserving trust and operating control, regardless of which rail sounds most modern. One market might need card, bank transfer, invoice and a local payment service provider. Another might need wallet or QR integration. A narrow global niche might include stablecoin access, and regulated B2B buyers might accept slower payments in exchange for stronger procurement evidence.

Data works the same way. Founders need to know what customer data they collect, where it is stored, which vendors touch it, which country rules apply, and whether enterprise buyers will accept the architecture. Regional integration helps most as those answers become more standardized, but each company still has to write its own down.

Sequence two or three markets as a corridor

The practical alternative to a whole-region plan is a corridor: a deliberately sequenced path through two or three markets where the same product burden can be tested with increasing difficulty.

A publication or advisory business might begin with Singapore-based operators who care about AI and regional expansion, test Malaysia or the Philippines for founder or C-suite demand, and move into Indonesia or Vietnam only once the offer has repeatable proof. A fintech infrastructure tool might start where regulation is clear and partners are available, then go to the market where the payment problem is most acute. Developer and AI workflow products can start with English-language regional teams and leave localization until the workflow has proved its value.

A corridor is faster because it stops a founder from learning five unrelated lessons at once. The first market validates the buyer and the promise, the second shows how much of the operation can be reused, and the third tests whether regional scale exists anywhere outside the deck.

Why sequencing suits small companies

Large companies can afford country managers, counsel, policy teams, reseller networks and slow localization. A small company's advantage has to come from sharper sequencing: pick a painful wedge, sell manually, learn from the first ten serious buyers, and build only the operating pieces that repeat.

Digital integration is useful even before it is finished, because it shows which standards are coming. Founders can build toward where policy, payments, identity and data flows are heading without waiting for harmonization to be complete. A market that looks small today may be the better choice if it teaches the company how to operate in the regional system that is forming.

Used loosely, "ASEAN" can stand in for a strategy that does not exist. The working version is to name the first buyer, choose the market that best tests that buyer, keep the operating burden reusable, and claim regional status only once there is proof.

Five questions before entering Southeast Asia

A founder should be able to answer these in plain language before committing to the region.

  1. Which country is the first test, and what specific commercial uncertainty does it resolve?
  2. Which buyer is being served, and why do they need this product now?
  3. Which operating pieces must be reusable in the next market: payments, invoicing, data, support, partners, content or compliance?
  4. Which local source of trust makes the first sale credible?
  5. What result would show that regional expansion is earned, and what result would force the company to stay narrow?

These questions are less exciting than a regional growth narrative and more likely to produce a business. ASEAN digital integration will help a company that has answered them, but the first market should still be the country whose result shows the company how to cross the next border.
