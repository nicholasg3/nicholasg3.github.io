Why AI Productivity Does Not Show Up

Plenty of people now use AI at work, but economy-wide evidence of higher productivity is thin. Operators still need local evidence that a given workflow now beats its pre-model baseline on output, cost, cycle time, or quality.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

Model capability climbed quickly in 2026 and investment followed, yet measured productivity gains remain uneven. For an operator, a model's skill at drafting or coding settles very little. What matters is whether a given workflow now beats its pre-model baseline on output, cost, cycle time, quality, or customer result.

The Financial Times has put the macro question to a wide audience: if AI is a major productivity shock, why does the economic data still obscure it? The Stanford Human-Centered AI Institute (HAI) 2026 AI Index records strong growth in investment, fast consumer uptake and clear progress on structured tasks. U.S. Census Bureau figures put business AI adoption near one fifth of U.S. firms early in 2026. Federal Reserve household data shows that one fourth of workers used generative AI at work in the prior month, though employer encouragement and quality gains showed up less often. McKinsey's surveys find that reported value comes from companies that redesign their workflows rather than those that merely license the tools.

So the question is one of proof, and strong companies measure productivity locally instead of waiting for a national statistic. Licenses, demos and prompt volume are inputs, and a count of them is not evidence that anything got faster or cheaper.

AI productivity appears when the work system changes enough that the business can measure the difference.

Adoption counts measure exposure

Adoption numbers are useful but coarse. A firm can log AI use when a team drafts messages or summarizes files, which tells you the staff have been exposed to the tools and leaves open whether the surrounding work system changed at all.

The Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey gives a steadier view. Between December 2025 and May 2026, overall U.S. business AI use ranged from 17% to 20%, with larger firms and information-sector firms running higher. Use is spreading, but at those levels it is nowhere near saturation.

Worker data is similarly mixed. The Federal Reserve figures put generative AI use at one fourth of workers in the prior month, with frequency varying by role. Many users report saving time; fewer report better quality or active support from their employer. Because productivity is a property of the whole system, a worker can reclaim ten private minutes while the organization records nothing and changes nothing.

Where productivity breaks

Between capability and a measured result sits a chain of weak links. The table below sets out each one.

Area Question False Positive
Adoption Who uses AI in real work, not only in pilots or personal experiments? Counting access, licenses, workshops, or Slack anecdotes as adoption.
Daily Use Which tasks now depend on AI often enough to change throughput? Celebrating occasional use that never alters the operating rhythm.
Workflow Redesign Which handoffs, approvals, interfaces, and roles changed because of AI? Adding a chatbot beside the old process and calling it transformation.
Measurement What output, quality, cost, time, or risk measure moved? Reporting hours saved without checking where the time went.
Learning Did the organization get better at the work, or only faster at producing drafts? Raising dependency while weakening review skill and domain judgment.
Capital Discipline Which AI spend, headcount plan, or vendor commitment is justified by the proof? Letting frontier excitement underwrite permanent cost without payback evidence.

Firms adopt the tool before they reorganize the work

AI often raises potential output before anyone reorganizes responsibility. The early gains arrive as scattered time savings: a faster draft, a quicker formula, a first-pass call summary. Individuals notice them, while the firm keeps its old meeting rhythm, approval steps and performance measures.

McKinsey's 2025 survey separates tool use from workflow redesign and links redesign to stronger reported financial impact, and that link largely decides whether adoption turns into an operating gain. When isolated tasks are automated and the process around them stays fixed, the bottleneck usually just moves to review, coordination or customer acceptance.

The San Francisco Fed's July 2026 review of generative AI research adds a complication. GenAI touches many occupations and tasks, yet adoption still differs among workers doing similar work, so exposure alone does not settle the outcome. Two employees in the same task setting can diverge, one building a lasting AI-assisted routine while the other changes little, and the explanation for that lies at least partly in how the work is managed.

Computing went through this before

Managers have seen this pattern before. In 1987 the economist Robert Solow, reviewing a book in the New York Times Book Review, wrote: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." In 1993 Erik Brynjolfsson called this the "productivity paradox of information technology" and weighed four explanations: mismeasured outputs and inputs, lags from learning and adjustment, profits that were redistributed or dissipated, and mismanagement.

Later research pointed to the organization. In 1996 Brynjolfsson and Lorin Hitt used firm-level data for 1987 to 1991 and found the picture looked different inside firms. In many of them, the restructuring and cost-cutting needed to realize the benefits of information systems spending had only recently begun. That same year Hitt and Brynjolfsson argued that productivity, profitability and consumer value are separate questions, and that the findings depended heavily on which question was asked and what data were used. In 2021 Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson described a "productivity J-curve." General purpose technologies such as AI need large complementary investments that are often intangible and poorly measured, so early gains are underestimated and later ones overestimated. AI may move faster or slower than computers did, and this history sets no timeline for it. It does suggest where to look: the gains came late, and they followed changes in the work.

What boards should ask instead

Boards often ask whether AI will raise productivity in general. A more useful question is which part of the business can produce a measurable productivity record inside one operating cycle.

By a record I mean a before-and-after result. In customer support that might be resolution time, escalation quality and review load; in software, shipped changes, defect rates and the time senior engineers spend reviewing. Sales might track pipeline quality and cycle length, and finance might track close-cycle compression and audit confidence.

AI does not have to prove itself everywhere at once, but vague claims should not get open-ended funding either. Without a defined proof, a company cannot distinguish productivity created by AI from work that has merely moved elsewhere, risk that has been hidden, or activity that only feels faster.

Start with the smallest workflow that can change

Closing the gap is a management job. Most AI discussion is about models, agents and chips, while productivity depends on queue design, handoffs, review rights, exception handling and measurement.

Pick the smallest workflow in which AI can alter how the work actually gets done. Good candidates have repeated volume, visible output, defined quality standards and a real cost of delay. Set a short proof window, and before starting require a baseline, a target, an estimate of review load and a stop rule. Expand only once the proof matches the actual process.

Handled this way, measurement can become an advantage. Competitors chasing broad adoption will show plenty of activity, while operators who insist on proof will learn which tasks merit automation or assistance, which need better data first, and which should stay under direct human control.

Checks for any AI productivity claim

Any claim that AI has raised productivity should survive these checks:

  1. Separate access or occasional use from daily use or redesigned work.
  2. Name what changed in the workflow besides the model.
  3. Name the metric that moved and its prior baseline.
  4. Show where the saved time, reduced cost or improved quality actually landed.
  5. Record any new review burden, failure mode or skill erosion.
  6. Name the owner who decides whether to scale, pause or stop.
  7. State what result would falsify the claim.

The last check matters most. If no result could count against a productivity program, the program is internal marketing.

AI may yet deliver a large productivity shock. If it does, the evidence will surface unevenly: first in structured tasks, then in redesigned workflows, and only later in aggregate statistics. Two errors follow from that: waiting for the macro signal before doing anything locally, and assuming the signal has already arrived. Until the statistics catch up, tie AI funding to local measurements, repeated productivity proofs and measurable operating gain.
