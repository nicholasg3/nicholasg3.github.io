How Often AI Work Should Run

AI use is moving from occasional prompting to recurring production work. Executives should manage when that work runs: what the system produces, when it produces it, where people step back in, and which jobs need redesigning rather than simply assisting.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

The first wave of AI adoption looked like a chat habit, which made it easy to misread. A worker asked a question, a model answered, and the company counted users and prompts and told itself a rough productivity story. That picture is already out of date. Anthropic's June 2026 Economic Index describes Claude usage shifting from a simple conversation between user and assistant toward longer-running agentic tasks. The report also classifies what users take away from a session: explanations, documents, reports, email drafts, code, analyses, plans, scripts, dashboards and other artifacts. The timing follows the week. Work use concentrates on weekdays and personal use rises on weekends. Business correspondence peaks inside the working day, while entrepreneurial conversations climb on Saturdays and Sundays.

So AI is settling into the rhythm of work. It turns out recurring outputs on daily and weekly cycles, and it changes what workers expect from their tools. For an operator, seat counts no longer answer the useful question, which is what rhythm of work AI is entering and who owns that rhythm once machines carry more of it.

The management skill in short supply is designing the schedule that AI work runs on.

What usage counts miss

Most adoption programs still start with seat activation, prompt volume and the number of employees trained. Those figures show that a tool has arrived in the organization but say little about whether it has changed the work.

A single prompt can produce a throwaway answer or a board memo. Daily users may be asking for trivia while an occasional user ships a pricing model. A code agent may run for an hour and return a working patch; a manager may hand off the first draft of an operating review and then spend just as long repairing its hidden assumptions. Junior analysts may learn faster, or they may quietly stop building judgment because every blank page now arrives filled. The measures worth tracking are what gets produced and where it is handed to a person.

Govern the outputs AI leaves behind

An artifact is whatever AI leaves behind: a document, a spreadsheet, a pull request, a customer reply, a dashboard, a decision note. Unlike a prompt log, which is hard to govern as business activity, an artifact can be examined. A company can record which category of work it serves, what human review it needs, which downstream system it touches and whether it becomes part of the operating record.

In the survey slice of Anthropic's report, documents and reports are the most common work output, followed by explanations, email drafts, analyses and summaries. Executives should take that seriously, because it means AI is already writing part of the company's internal memory. At that scale, controls placed only at the edge of the model are not enough; governance has to sit in the workflow, at the point where outputs feed into decisions.

Five controls for an AI work schedule

A workable schedule for AI work has five controls.

Schedule item Question Bad pattern
Artifact What concrete output does AI produce, and where does it live after the session? Counting usage while ignoring the quality and destination of the work product.
Rhythm Is this daily, weekly, event-triggered, seasonal, or ad hoc work? Automating a recurring process without naming its calendar and volume.
Handoff Where does the human re-enter: before drafting, after drafting, before action, or only on exception? Letting AI produce finished-looking work with no explicit ownership point.
Review Burden How much human checking does the artifact create, and who is qualified to do it? Calling work faster because generation got faster while review queues expand.
Job Redesign Which responsibilities move, which skills compound, and which junior-learning loops need protection? Treating role change as an HR afterthought after workflow change has already happened.

The list is deliberately mundane, meant to move AI out of the demo and into the operating calendar.

Test AI against the busy weeks

Work has a rhythm and AI inherits it. Anthropic's cadence data shows personal use rising on weekends, work use concentrated during the week, and different clusters of requests peaking at different hours. No company needs to copy Claude's pattern, but the data does show that AI usage breaks into separate patterns tied to how people live and work, which a single aggregate hides.

Review a customer-support assistant against ticket surges, since average daily prompts will flatter it. Test a finance agent around close, audit, tax and planning deadlines, and a sales assistant around campaign windows and quarter-end. Judge a coding agent on pull-request throughput, defect rate and review load across the working week instead of on a polished one-off demo. A system that works at 2 p.m. on a calm Wednesday and fails in the Monday morning queue is not ready for production. If it saves drafting time but piles review work up on Friday, the load has simply moved to Friday.

Name the mode at each step

A lazy version of the debate asks whether work will be automated or augmented. Real operations rarely divide that neatly, and one workflow often contains both. AI may gather sources unsupervised, draft under light supervision, ask for a human call on ambiguous claims, and then wait for a manager before anything is published. A coding flow may write code, run the tests and propose a patch, yet still need a person to approve the merge.

Anthropic distinguishes modes such as directive delegation, feedback loops, task iteration, learning and validation. The vocabulary helps because "AI-assisted" on its own describes almost nothing, and each workflow should state which mode is permitted at which step. The danger lies in automation nobody has named. A company can accept heavy automation in a bounded extraction task while still requiring human judgment on pricing, diagnosis, hiring, legal, security or anything that affects customers, and the schedule should write that difference down.

What happens to junior staff

This question cannot wait. In Anthropic's June 2026 survey, more than a third of respondents expected AI to be able to do most or nearly all of their work tasks within twelve months. They were particularly worried about junior colleagues: more than a third put the probability of a junior colleague losing a job in the next year above 60 percent. Yet large majorities also reported productivity gains, and many said AI was helping them learn or making their skills more valuable.

None of this is a clean forecast. The survey is not representative of the labor market as a whole; it reflects Claude users, who skew toward technical and managerial occupations. It does capture the tension managers face, though: AI can make experienced workers faster while shortening the apprenticeship that produces the next generation of experienced workers.

Job redesign belongs in the schedule for that reason. If AI writes the first draft, does the junior analyst still learn to structure an argument? If it produces the research brief, who learns to judge sources? If it handles routine code changes, where does a new engineer develop a feel for debugging? And if it summarizes customer calls, who catches the weak signal the summary dropped? A serious AI strategy has to protect those learning loops deliberately, because a busy team will not keep them going by accident.

A checklist for each workflow

For every AI workflow, a manager should keep a short schedule covering six points.

  1. Output: what is produced, and which system stores it?
  2. Frequency: how often does the work recur, and what events cause spikes?
  3. Mode: is AI learning, validating, iterating, taking direction or running a feedback loop?
  4. Review: what must a qualified person check before the output affects another person, system or decision?
  5. Learning: which skill must the human keep practicing rather than hand over entirely?
  6. Escalation: what error, uncertainty, cost, permission or confidence threshold stops the machine and names an owner?

Without something like this, AI turns into invisible middle management, assigning tasks, shaping drafts, creating records and passing work between people with no explicit operating model behind any of it.

Set goals for workflows instead of usage

Executives should stop asking for broad AI adoption as if adoption were the result they wanted. It is a precondition for better work. A more useful target reads something like: "By the end of the quarter, three recurring workflows will have AI-produced artifacts, named review owners, measured review burden, explicit escalation rules, and protected learning loops for junior staff." Next to a company-wide usage figure it looks modest, but it obliges the organization to know what actually changed.

Seven questions before expanding a workflow

Before an AI workflow is extended, require answers to these.

  1. What does AI produce?
  2. Which recurring rhythm does that output enter?
  3. Where does a person re-enter the workflow?
  4. How much review work does it create, and who is qualified to do it?
  5. Which downstream system, customer, employee or decision can the output affect?
  6. Which skill might wither if the work is delegated too completely?
  7. What stop rule hands the work back to human ownership?

If the answers are vague, what the company has is a usage story, and the operating model for AI has yet to be built.

What the adoption data shows

The new adoption data shows AI work settling into a cycle of peak hours, weekends and deadlines, with outputs and handoffs that shift what people expect from their jobs. All of that tells a manager more than a single usage rate does. Governing the model, buying seats and counting prompts no longer cover it, because the way work moves through the week is changing, and each company now has to decide how its own work should move.
