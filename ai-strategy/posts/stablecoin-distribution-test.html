Should a Small Business Accept Stablecoins?

Stablecoins are moving from crypto infrastructure into payment operations. For a small digital business, the question is whether accepting them changes who can pay, how fast cash clears, and how much operating work the founder is willing to take on.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

Every payment rail arrives with a promise. For cards it was convenience and for crypto an internet-native money layer, while wallets sold mobile conversion and bank transfers sold lower fees. Stablecoins make a more sober pitch: digital dollars that move around the clock and settle quickly across borders, without the full machinery of correspondent banking.

That pitch has left the crypto-native exchanges. The United States now has a federal payment-stablecoin law, and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) have been building rulemaking around bank participation. Singapore has a single-currency stablecoin framework. Visa has pushed stablecoin settlement deeper into its network, and Coinbase, Checkout.com, PPRO, Nium, and other payment firms are trying to turn stablecoin acceptance into one more payment method inside an existing stack. For a founder or a small publisher, a tempting conclusion follows. If cards are annoying and Substack or Stripe takes work to set up, why not put a wallet address on the page and gate access with stablecoins? Sometimes that is a sensible local experiment. More often it adds a way to pay without adding any buyers.

The question to ask of stablecoins is whether the rail reaches a buyer you could not otherwise serve, at an operating cost you can actually manage.

What stablecoin payments now offer

The strongest case for stablecoin payments is operational. Buyers can hold dollar-denominated value and settle outside banking hours. A merchant can sometimes cut chargeback exposure and settlement delay at once, and simplify cross-border payouts along the way. Platforms gain an option for customers who already keep their money in stablecoins.

Recent market signals support the case. The GENIUS Act gave the United States a statutory framework for payment stablecoins, covering permitted issuers, reserve requirements, disclosures, and Bank Secrecy Act obligations. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) took its own route: a regulated label for single-currency stablecoins built around reserve, redemption, disclosure, and prudential conditions. Visa describes stablecoin settlement as part of treasury and settlement modernization and makes no claim that it will replace the consumer card experience. Coinbase's 2026 commerce messaging points the same way. It sells stablecoin payments to platforms and payment service providers as the familiar primitives of authorization, capture, refund, webhook, settlement, and reporting. Payment companies are being asked to hide enough complexity that a merchant can accept digital-dollar demand without rebuilding its checkout or its treasury operations, which means consumers need not become crypto power users for the rail to matter.

What a bare wallet address leaves the founder to handle

The easy mistake is to treat stablecoins as a revenue shortcut. A wallet address can receive money, but the merchant is then left alone with identification, refunds, taxes, sanctions screening, reconciliation, and support. A manual stablecoin gate can still help with a tiny early audience, because it shows that a reader or customer wants the product enough to push a payment through some friction, and international buyers who already hold USD Coin or Tether may prefer it. It works when the seller knows the customer and the product is low-risk, with a simple refund policy and access granted by hand, say by a reply or a manual allowlist.

Opened to the public, the same setup is fragile. The founder now owns questions that Stripe, Substack, Shopify, bank rails, app stores, or enterprise payment providers usually absorb: who the customer is and which jurisdiction they sit in, what happens when someone sends the wrong token or uses the wrong network, how access is revoked after a missed renewal, and what to do about issuer freezes, bridge failures, chain congestion, and compliance flags.

Four commercial facts the rail has to change

Judge stablecoins by what they do for distribution. The rail is worth adding only if it changes one of four commercial facts:

Test Useful Signal False Positive
Buyer access Customers who could not or would not pay by card, bank transfer, or platform checkout now pay. Existing customers switch rails but total demand does not increase.
Settlement value Faster clearing, weekend availability, or cross-border settlement materially improves cash management. The founder saves a fee but spends more time on reconciliation and support.
Product fit The product naturally serves crypto-native, global, contractor, creator, or agentic-commerce users. The product is ordinary SaaS with a crypto checkout bolted on for novelty.
Operating control Custody, refunds, tax records, access gating, sanctions screening, and accounting can be handled cleanly. The founder receives payments but cannot run a professional back office.

What the arrival of large payment firms shows

The entry of large payment and exchange firms supports a narrower claim than "every small merchant should accept stablecoins tomorrow". The market is trying to professionalize the interface between onchain settlement and ordinary business operations. Customers want to buy products without managing payment rails, and merchants have no wish to become compliance desks, so the stablecoin products that succeed will be the ones that reduce the number of crypto-specific decisions a normal business has to make.

Founders should therefore be wary of building the first version too low in the stack. A direct wallet payment proves only that a buyer was willing to overcome friction, which may be enough for a private paid-note experiment. Provider-mediated checkout shows something more useful for a business that wants to scale: that the rail can fit inside normal commerce.

How regulators are treating stablecoins

Regulation is becoming part of the product. In the United States, stablecoins are being pulled into issuer permissioning, reserve disclosure, supervision, and anti-money-laundering obligations. The MAS framework in Singapore tries to mark out a class of single-currency stablecoins that meet local requirements. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) remains more skeptical, warning that stablecoins can create monetary, financial-stability, integrity, and sovereignty risks if they grow without the safeguards of the two-tier monetary system.

A founder does not need to settle the central-bank debate to draw the practical conclusion, which is that accepting stablecoins offers no escape from regulation. The more successful the rail becomes, the more it will be wrapped in regulated issuers and licensed intermediaries, along with the screening and record-keeping that come with them. A business that wants the benefit should assume from the first day that customer records and due diligence on providers will matter.

How to run a manual pilot

For a tiny paid publication or micro-SaaS, a manual stablecoin pilot can still be rational if it is narrow and easy to reverse, with every term written down:

  1. Customer: who specifically prefers or needs to pay in stablecoins?
  2. Product: what access is granted, for how long, and how renewal works.
  3. Asset and network: one of each, with no improvising across tokens.
  4. Operating owner: who reconciles payments and grants access, including refunds and a record of each transaction.
  5. Stop rule: the support load, failure rate or level of compliance uncertainty at which the experiment ends, or the demand level below which it does.

A pilot like this is closer to a concierge sale than to a subscription system, and its job is to tell the founder whether a payment rail expands demand before any time goes into a platform integration or a custody setup.

Where stablecoins fit, and in what order

Stablecoins will matter most where moving money is itself part of the product: cross-border contractors, creator payouts, commerce platforms, emerging-market users holding digital dollars, treasury-heavy marketplaces, and agentic microtransactions. They will matter less where customers already pay easily by card or disputes are frequent, and less again where trust rests on a familiar checkout brand.

For a founder, then, the decision is about sequencing. Use Substack, Stripe or a familiar card checkout when trust and ease count for more than a new rail. Run a stablecoin pilot when the first customers already hold their money in that rail, or when the payment constraint really does block distribution. Move to provider-mediated stablecoin acceptance once volume or risk justifies a professional back office.

The rail is becoming institutional enough that skeptics should keep watching it, though a wallet payment on its own still falls well short of a business system. Before switching anything on, a founder should be able to name the buyer the rail reaches and the extra work it creates. It also helps to decide in advance at what volume the manual process gets handed to a provider.
