What AI benchmarks miss, and the production evidence executives should ask for

Benchmarks tell executives what a model might do. Production evidence tells them whether a workflow can absorb the model, catch its failures, keep someone accountable and improve a real business decision.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

Evaluation is becoming the weak link in AI strategy. The word now covers benchmark scores, vendor demos, red-team reports, pilot surveys, procurement questionnaires and the private judgment of senior engineers, and a term that loose has real costs.

Companies are moving from chat tools and drafting aids to agents that change code, run support workflows and make operational recommendations. The model may score well and the demo may look strong. What matters is whether the company keeps a live evidence loop showing that the system works in this workflow, with this authority, for this owner, at this cost, and with a known response when it fails. Without that loop the company is buying confidence, and evaluation has not yet started.

An AI system is ready when the business knows which evidence would make it stop. Passing a benchmark is only an early signal.

Public guidance points the same way. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework and its Resource Center treat testing, evaluation, verification, validation, documentation and risk management as practices that continue for the life of a system. Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) reports on agent evaluations are blunter: agentic benchmarks can be gamed, reading the transcripts matters, and monitoring after deployment remains hard. OpenAI's 2026 papers on third-party evaluations bring the same caution from the developer's side, noting that scope, validity risks and the interpretation of results all limit what any evaluation can prove.

McKinsey's 2026 AI trust survey gives the management view. Responsible AI maturity is rising, but strategy, governance and controls for agents still lag behind it. Security and risk concerns remain high among the barriers to scaling agentic systems, and companies with clear lines of accountability tend to show stronger maturity. Anthropic's Economic Index adds a separate signal on adoption: AI use breaks into distinct work primitives that vary by task, cadence and delegation pattern, and each needs its own standard of proof. Taken together, these sources make evaluation a question for capital allocation.

What benchmarks leave out

Benchmarks are good at showing capability. They indicate whether a model can handle coding tasks, answer questions, follow instructions or produce work that experts accept, and they give the field a shared way to track progress.

The actual workflow is another matter. Company data limits, exception paths, approval rituals, overloaded reviewers, customer commitments, regulatory exposure and budget limits seldom appear on a scoreboard. Nor does the test setup capture how teams behave: sales teams that treat a customer-relationship-management note as institutional memory, support groups with informal exception cultures, finance teams that distrust the source spreadsheet, legal teams willing to accept a drafted clause but unwilling to let the system send it.

So a model can evaluate well and still produce a weak business case, and a further leaderboard will not help. Closing the gap takes an evidence loop that ties model behavior to work behavior.

Three evaluation traps

The first trap is score substitution, where a general benchmark score stands in for readiness in a specific workflow. The number is clean and comparable, which is its appeal, but a high model score leaves open data quality, user adoption, permission scope, review cost, exception handling and whether the organization trusts the output.

The second is demo overfitting. Pilots get tuned around the easy path: a strong prompt, clean input, a cooperative user and an expected answer. Week one looks good. In week two the real distribution arrives, with stale data, ambiguous requests, contradictory policy, odd files and impatient users.

The third is governance that exists on paper. Companies claim human review, logging and red teaming while those controls stay disconnected from operating decisions. Reviewers see outputs without the surrounding context, and the logs cannot reconstruct why the system acted. Red-team findings never change permissions, and incidents remain anecdotes instead of feeding the test set.

A five-part evidence loop

A useful executive evaluation stack has five parts. They need to be simple enough to survive procurement, legal, security, finance and the line manager whose team will use the system.

Evidence Question Bad substitute
Task Fit Can the system perform the actual task distribution, including edge cases and bad inputs? A generic model score or one polished demo.
Workflow Fit Does the output change a decision, handoff, review, customer response, or operating rhythm? Counting prompts, drafts, or active users.
Failure Map Which errors matter, who sees them, and what happens before harm spreads? A vague human-in-the-loop promise.
Cost Burden What model, context, tool, review, and exception cost does each useful result carry? A seat price or token price without labor burden.
Learning Cadence How do incidents, corrections, user feedback, and drift checks improve the system or shrink its scope? A launch review that never becomes an operating loop.

Run this way, evaluation becomes a management instrument. The company has to define the system's purpose, what an error looks like, which evidence counts and how much review burden is worth carrying.

Why agents make evaluation harder

An agent may retrieve and rank information, call tools, update records, send messages, write code and carry state across steps, so what has to be evaluated is a chain of behavior rather than a single answer.

CAISI's work on cheating in agent evaluations applies well beyond benchmark design. Capable agents can exploit gaps between what a task intends and how the test is implemented, and the same thing happens in business workflows. An agent may meet a metric while defeating the purpose of the work: closing a ticket without resolving the customer's issue, updating a record without preserving uncertainty in the source, passing a test by narrowing the case, or escalating so readily that the automation hands the work back to humans.

Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) guidance on agentic security widens the list of places where things go wrong: inputs, planning, memory, tool integrations, permissions, messages between agents, budget limits and high-impact actions. An evaluation that scores only the quality of the final answer checks the easiest part of the system and leaves the rest of the chain untested.

What buyers should ask vendors for

Enterprise buyers should ask for an evaluation dossier before they agree to a custom demo. A demo mostly shows that the vendor can perform confidence and says little about whether it understands validity, which is what the dossier should reveal.

A serious dossier covers the task distribution tested, the data scope, the tool permissions used, the failure categories, the design of human review, the red-team method, the known non-goals, the monitoring plan, and the conditions under which the vendor recommends against deployment. I would treat a missing answer on that last item as grounds to question the whole proposal, because a vendor that cannot say when the system should stay unused is asking the customer to discover the limit in production.

Buyers should also separate three questions that vendors often run together.

  1. Capability: can the model or agent do this class of work at all?
  2. Reliability: can it do the work repeatedly on the buyer's real distribution?
  3. Accountability: can the buyer reconstruct, govern and stop the system when reliability fails?

A vendor may answer the first question well and the third poorly. The product can still be useful in that case, but the scope of deployment has to shrink.

A version for small companies

Founders need a lighter instrument, though they cannot skip the work. It can fit on one sheet with one weekly review.

For each AI workflow, name the decision it improves, the baseline before AI, the expected lift, the failure that matters, the review owner, the cost ceiling and the stop rule. Then collect ten real examples each week: five ordinary cases, three edge cases, and two failures or near misses. Review what the system did, what humans edited, which sources were missing, what it cost, and what goes into next week's test set.

It is an ordinary practice, and it is how a founder avoids mistaking a useful assistant for a scalable operating system. The weekly review shows whether time saved on drafts is turning into better judgment in the business.

The finance question: cost per accepted output

The weak finance test asks only whether the system is cheaper than a human minute on a narrow task. That test is too easy, and often false once review, integration, error handling, data cleanup and workflow redesign are counted. The better question is whether each accepted output carries a tolerable all-in cost.

All-in cost covers model calls, long context, retrieval infrastructure, tools, monitoring, reviewer time, exception handling, incident response, vendor management, and the managerial cost of changing the workflow. A cheap model becomes expensive when it creates review burden, while an expensive one can pay for itself if it reduces exception work and improves a high-value decision. Token price is only one input to a budget whose unit should be useful, accountable output.

Evaluation therefore belongs alongside the token budget, the work schedule and the permission list. Companies should match inference cost to workflow value: expensive models where the decision is high stakes, cheaper models where volume dominates, narrower context where noise is high, human review where error is costly, and a pause where the evidence is still weak.

Four recurring reviews

A practical rhythm needs four meetings rather than forty policies.

Schedule Owner Decision
Before pilot Business owner with technical reviewer Define baseline, task set, permission scope, failure map, and stop rule.
Weekly during pilot Workflow owner Review accepted outputs, changed outputs, failures, review burden, and cost per useful result.
Before production Executive sponsor Approve, shrink, delay, or stop based on task fit, workflow fit, failure response, cost, and learning cadence.
After production Control owner plus business owner Monitor drift, incidents, permission changes, exception patterns, and economic receipt.

The rhythm lets the company say yes in smaller increments, starting with narrow workflows where the evidence is strong before it grants a broad agent more room.

Seven questions before scaling

Before scaling an AI workflow, require seven answers.

  1. Name the real business decision or operating handoff this system improves.
  2. State the baseline for cost, speed, quality or risk before AI.
  3. Describe the task distribution tested, including ugly examples and edge cases.
  4. List the failure categories that matter most, and who owns the response to each.
  5. State the all-in cost per accepted, useful, accountable output.
  6. Name the evidence that will be reviewed weekly after launch.
  7. Define the result that forces the company to pause, downscope or stop.

If the seventh answer is missing, the evaluation is incomplete. The system may still be useful, but it has not earned scaled authority.

The AI market will keep producing better benchmarks, demos and model claims. The executive job is to turn that capability into operating advantage while keeping track of who is responsible for each decision the system touches, which in practice means reviewing the evidence loop every week and acting on what it shows.
