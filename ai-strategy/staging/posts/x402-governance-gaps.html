What x402 Leaves Out, and Who Fills the Gaps

Coinbase's x402 protocol solves the payment step for machine-to-machine commerce: an HTTP request meets a 402 status code, an agent attaches a signed payment, a facilitator confirms it on-chain, the resource unlocks. What happens when that payment is wrong — sent to the wrong party, for the wrong amount, by an agent that misread its own instructions — is not in the protocol. That gap will not stay empty for long, and payment history says whoever fills it first tends to keep it.

x402 revives HTTP's dormant 402 "Payment Required" status code: a server asks for payment, an agent attaches a signed stablecoin transfer, a facilitator — Coinbase runs the reference implementation — broadcasts the transaction and confirms settlement before the resource is served. Coinbase's documentation and independent explainers converge on what happens next: nothing. No chargeback path, no dispute window, no merchant pull-back once the facilitator confirms. Payment is final the moment it clears; refunds, if a seller offers them, live in application logic or optional add-ons like x402r.

That is a defensible design choice. A protocol that specified disputes, KYC, and authority up front would be heavier and would have to pick sides on jurisdiction and identity questions no payments primitive can settle for the whole internet. But "we left it out" is not "it stays unfilled." Somebody always writes the rules for a payment gone wrong. The question is who, and how they get paid for it.

A minimal protocol is a governance choice as much as an engineering one — it decides who gets to design the layer above it, not whether that layer gets designed.

The metadata gap previews the identity gap

A small case shows how casually the missing layers get filled. In April 2026, researcher Vladimir Stantchev published "Hardening x402: PII-Safe Agentic Payments via Pre-Execution Metadata Filtering" on arXiv, documenting that every x402 payment request carries a resource URL, a description, and a reason string that travel to the facilitator before settlement, with neither party typically bound by a data-processing agreement. His fix, an open-source middleware called presidio-hardened-x402, redacts identifying data before it leaves the agent. It is evidence of the pattern: x402 said nothing about who is accountable for that metadata, so a third party built the accountability layer afterward, with no requirement that any facilitator adopt it.

Identity is the harder version of the same problem. Agent payments will eventually need to answer "who authorized this, on whose behalf" with more than a wallet signature. FATF's Recommendation 16 — the Travel Rule — already requires virtual asset service providers to exchange verified originator and beneficiary information above a threshold. x402 does not touch that layer; a facilitator moving real money at scale sits on top of Travel Rule compliance built by someone else, and that someone becomes load-bearing infrastructure for everyone who transacts through them.

Two answers already shipping, at different altitudes

Google's Agent Payments Protocol, built with more than sixty companies including Coinbase, Mastercard, and PayPal, sits above settlement. AP2 chains three signed mandates — an Intent Mandate delegating authority with explicit constraints, a Cart Mandate locking in the exact cart and price, and a Payment Mandate the network sees — giving merchants an audit trail for authorization and fraud accountability. Settlement can still run over stablecoins through an x402 extension Google co-developed with Coinbase, the Ethereum Foundation, and MetaMask: less a competitor to x402 than the mandate layer x402 left out, wrapped around it.

The Agentic Commerce Protocol, built by Stripe and OpenAI, took the opposite path: fewer open questions, faster to production. ACP already powers Instant Checkout in ChatGPT — Etsy live at 2025 launch, Shopify merchants including Glossier, Vuori, and Spanx following — because Stripe owns the payments relationship end to end and did not need a coalition first. x402 stays open-source and, by most comparisons circulating in the space, mostly developer experiments; ACP is proprietary-adjacent and live; AP2 is a coalition standard still early. Convergence shows at the edges — Stripe supports both its Modern Payment Protocol and x402, and AP2 ships an x402 extension — but that is compatible with concentration at the center, since the facilitator, mandate issuer, and checkout owner need not be the same party.

Singapore is treating this as infrastructure, not a feature

MAS's BLOOM initiative, launched to extend wholesale settlement into tokenized bank liabilities and regulated stablecoins, names "agentic payments" — AI agents executing transactions within pre-defined limits — as an explicit workstream, with Coinbase and DBS attached specifically to it. The rest of the initiative splits distribution and clearing (Circle, DBS, OCBC, Partior, Stripe, UOB) from programmable compliance controls (Ant International, StraitsX). That split is a tell: MAS is not asking whether agents should pay each other, it is asking which regulated institution owns the controls when they do — a governance move, not a pilot.

Why the card-network precedent should worry protocol designers

Visa and Mastercard's chargeback rules are not written by merchants, issuing banks, or any neutral body — exclusively by the networks. The 1974 Fair Credit Billing Act gave US cardholders a right to dispute charges but specified no rulebook; each network built its own reason codes, evidence standards, and arbitration process, and everyone else operates inside whatever version the network publishes. An accessible front end (any merchant can plug into either network) coexists with total concentration of dispute authority in two private rulebooks — the same design working as intended for whoever wrote the back end.

Email shows the shape without any dispute mechanism at all. SMTP is a neutral, unowned protocol; anyone can run a mail server. Gmail still holds close to half of the US email-provider market and a clear majority of webmail, per Statista's tracking of US respondents. Openness at the protocol layer did not stop concentration at the facilitation layer — it moved the leverage point from who controls SMTP (nobody) to whose inbox people check (mostly Google's). x402 being open-source settles nothing about whether facilitation concentrates the same way; an open protocol invites many implementers to compete on convenience while only a few can plausibly run the trust infrastructure — KYC, fraud monitoring, dispute handling — a facilitator needs once real disputes show up.

The part economics predicted and payments people rediscovered

Nick Szabo argued in 1999 that micropayments failed for cognitive, not technical, reasons: "mental transaction costs," the overhead of deciding whether two cents of content is worth two cents, dwarfs the computational cost of moving the money. Clay Shirky's 2000 essay against micropayments made the same point from the other side: no transaction is a "no-brainer" for a human, because an amount large enough to matter to the seller is large enough that the buyer pauses.

Agents remove exactly that cost — an agent feels no hassle deciding whether an API call is worth a fraction of a cent; it executes a policy. That is why x402-style protocols work now in a way they could not in 2000. But removing the decision cost cuts both ways: nothing stops an agent from comparison-shopping every purchase instantly, which makes price competition among facilitators frictionless in a way it never was for human checkout. A facilitator's moat will not come from payment-layer switching costs — agents do not have those the way people do. It comes from whichever non-payment layer is hardest to switch: the dispute rulebook, the identity check, the compliance relationship a regulator like MAS actually trusts. That is where the Visa and Mastercard lesson applies, and it is the layer x402 says nothing about.

The governance question underneath the engineering one

None of this argues against x402, or predicts that Coinbase's implementation will end up owning the dispute layer the way Visa and Mastercard own chargebacks. AP2's coalition model, MAS's regulator-convened workstream, and Stripe and OpenAI's already-shipping ACP are three different bets on who should hold that pen, and none has won yet. What the boundary-resources literature on platforms predicts, and what card-network history already shows, is that the layer left undefined does not stay neutral: whoever builds the interface that resolves a bad transaction ends up with more power over the ecosystem than whoever wrote the payment header, because everyone else has to build against that interface. x402's minimalism was a genuine engineering virtue — it shipped, it works, and it stays open-source where its closest competitors do not. Whether that minimalism turns into governance abdicated to whoever moves fastest, or governance deliberately left for institutions like MAS to convene properly, is worth asking before agent-to-agent commerce runs real disputed transactions at scale — not after.
