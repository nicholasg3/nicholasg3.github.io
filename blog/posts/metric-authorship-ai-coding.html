If success means task completion, the score may track who defines the task

Before that finding becomes a hiring slogan, check what the scoreboard is actually counting.

A finding has been circulating in the agent-coding conversation: in large session logs, managers slightly outperform engineers at getting agentic coding tools to finish work. The claim flatters people who write tickets and demoralizes people who write systems. Before it becomes a hiring or training slogan, check what the scoreboard counts.

When "good at AI coding" means verifiable completion of defined tasks, skill at using the tool partly collapses into skill at defining what counts as done. The metric scores performance and, at the same time, selects a kind of hero.

What the public story is selling

The public story is simple. Agentic tools reward people who can specify goals, constraints, and acceptance tests. Managers already speak that language; engineers live in implementation detail; therefore managers win. The story travels because it is neat. It also matches how product organizations already want to revalue "product sense" over craft.

Handle consensus around the finding with care. In one capture of reply traffic to the headline claim, dissent was almost absent. Missing argument is weak confirmation. Often the frame simply flatters the audience that amplifies it.

What the metric is actually doing

Completion metrics need a definition of done. Someone has to write the prompt, the ticket, the evaluation setup, or the checklist the agent is scored against. Specification skill is therefore baked into the measurement apparatus. Espeland and Sauder’s work on commensuration is useful here: once a practice is scored, the score reshapes what counts as skill. Goodhart’s law is the short form of the same worry. When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a neutral description.

In agentic coding, that problem is sharper than usual. Setting up the agent to succeed is close to defining success. A person who writes a crisp, verifiable task description is both playing the game and designing the rules. A person working on messy, partially specified systems may post a lower completion rate even when the work is harder.

If you score… You tend to reward… You tend to miss…
Task completion rate Clear specs, narrow scope, verifiable endpoints Ambiguous systems work, judgment under incomplete requirements
Time to first green test Speed at scaffolding and tool fluency Design quality, long-horizon maintainability
Human revision load after the agent Outputs that need little cleanup Whether the task was worth doing
Incident rate after merge Restraint and review discipline Productivity theater that never ships risk

A concrete scene

Two people open the same agent. One pastes a ticket: add a feature flag, wire it to an existing config service, cover it with three integration tests that already exist in the repo. That run finishes. The other is debugging a race that only appears under production-like load, with partial logs and no agreed definition of "fixed." Patches look plausible and fail on the next run. On a completion dashboard, person one is the hero and person two looks slow. Completion is reported accurately, and the dashboard still hides what kind of work it can see.

What would make the claim scientific

To separate "good at agents" from "good at writing the score," a study would need to hold the task definition constant across roles, measure specification quality as its own variable, or score outcomes that the tool user did not write. Without that separation, manager-outperforms-engineer is partly a finding about who sits closer to the metric’s authorship.

Managers can still be excellent agent users. The headline is still weak evidence for replacing engineering depth with ticket writing. Specification skill is real. Craft that resists a clean checklist is real too.

Closing

Before you reorganize a team around "who is good at AI coding," ask who wrote the definition of good. When the same people look best on the scoreboard, the ranking is partly a self-portrait of the scoreboard's authors.
