Governance

The AI Evidence Packet

AI procurement is moving from demo judgment to evidence judgment. Before a company buys an agent, model, copilot, or automation layer, it should know which claims have proof, which risks are owned, and which failures would force a rollback.

The first wave of enterprise AI purchases rewarded quick answers and smooth demos. A model replied fast. A copilot produced text. A vendor presented a workflow that appeared simpler and cheaper. Early tests often stopped there.

That standard no longer holds. AI now sits inside contracts, customer workflows, internal decisions, regulated files, security perimeters, and board materials. Procurement questions have shifted from whether a product can impress during a meeting to whether supporting evidence remains after the meeting ends.

Ordinary SaaS purchases already require clear terms on uptime, data handling, access rules, logs, support, pricing, and exit rights. AI purchases leave the same teams facing a less defined object that may rest on an outside model, shifting prompts, a vector store, customer data, tool rights, human oversight, vendor monitoring, and output that changes with time.

The practical AI procurement test is not whether the demo works. It is whether the evidence packet survives contact with the workflow.

The New Buyer Problem

Standards groups have already described what serious oversight requires. The National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Risk Management Framework structures the work around governance, mapping, measurement, and management. Its profile for generative systems adds specific concerns around synthetic content, data leaks, model behavior, misuse, and evaluation limits. European Commission guidance on the AI Act converts documentation, model limits, risk checks, incident reports, copyright rules, and security measures into obligations that reach both providers and users.

Security directives move in the same direction. The Open Worldwide Application Security Project list of large language model risks includes prompt injection, unsafe output handling, supply chain gaps, excessive agency, disclosure of sensitive data, and overreliance. Guidance from the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre stresses that security ownership, transparency, and secure design must apply to AI systems. The CAISI 2026 request for information on agent security highlights new exposure when model outputs connect to actions, credentials, external data, and live environments.

Consumer rules add a further requirement. The Federal Trade Commission treats AI claims the same as any other: they need evidence, and the use of AI does not remove liability for deception or unfair practices. The precise line differs by sector, yet the operating rule stays the same. A buyer should refuse any vendor assertion that the vendor could not defend before a regulator, insurer, customer, or board.

What The Packet Must Contain

The evidence packet is not a thick compliance file. It is the smallest set of materials that lets an executive judge whether a system belongs in live operations rather than a scripted demonstration.

Evidence Layer What The Buyer Needs Weak Answer
Claim evidence The benchmark, eval, customer result, or controlled test behind each material product claim. "Our model is state of the art" without saying for which task, population, constraint, or failure cost.
Workflow fit The live process, user, data source, review step, and success metric the system is designed to change. A generic productivity claim that never names the operating bottleneck.
Risk ownership Which party owns privacy, security, hallucination, IP, regulatory, user-review, and incident duties. "Shared responsibility" without a responsibility matrix.
Change control How model, prompt, retrieval, policy, and tool-permission changes are logged, tested, and communicated. Silent product changes that alter behavior inside a buyer's critical workflow.
Failure response The escalation path, rollback rule, incident notice, audit trail, and human override for serious failures. Support-language promises that do not tell the buyer what happens when the system causes damage.

These layers create a clear division. Some evidence must come from the vendor. The rest must come from the buyer. The vendor can document model limits, security measures, change procedures, data practices, evaluation methods, and known failure points. The buyer still sets the workflow scope, the autonomy line, the review steps, the performance measure, and the local cost of error.

Procurement therefore cannot hand AI decisions to legal or IT alone. Legal can shape contract terms. Security can examine controls. IT can assess architecture. Only the operating owner can identify which errors would matter, which person stays accountable, and whether the productivity gain justifies the added dependence.

The Vendor Test

A capable vendor treats the evidence packet as part of the sales process rather than an obstacle. Strong AI suppliers understand that trust grows when implementation risks are made explicit.

Before any move past a pilot, buyers should ask the vendor these six questions:

  1. Which product claims rest on task-specific tests, and which remain directional or experimental?
  2. What data enters the system, where does it reside, and how is it applied to training, improvement, monitoring, or support?
  3. Which model, retrieval method, prompt, tool, or policy change can affect output quality after the contract is signed?
  4. What controls address prompt injection, excessive agency, unsafe tool use, and sensitive data exposure?
  5. What notice, logs, rollback options, and export paths exist if the workflow must halt?
  6. Which duties stay with the buyer even when the product performs as specified?

When answers are missing, the deal may still suit a narrow test. It should not move into production infrastructure. The practical response is often a tighter scope, reduced permissions, added human review, or a contract that preserves flexibility instead of locking in dependence.

The Buyer Test

Buyers also fall short. Many teams press vendors for evidence while producing none of their own. They request full model documentation yet cannot name the target workflow. They seek low error rates yet cannot define which errors count. They want indemnity yet cannot identify who will review outputs. They call for governance dashboards without naming who can halt rollout.

The buyer's packet needs five local details:

  1. The workflow in scope, including inputs, outputs, users, and escalation steps.
  2. The autonomy boundary: recommend, draft, rank, execute, or trigger external action.
  3. The error budget: which errors are acceptable, which need review, and which are not allowed.
  4. The business result: time saved, revenue protected, risk lowered, retention raised, or learning sped up.
  5. The stop rule: the metric, incident, cost change, quality drop, or adoption signal that ends the effort.

The exercise appears formal only when speed is mistaken for lack of definition. A one-page packet can accelerate a deal by removing false certainty. The vendor knows what to prove. The buyer knows what to monitor. Finance sees the obligation. Security sees the boundary. The executive sponsor sees whether the project is an operational investment or a publicity exercise.

Why This Becomes Strategic

The evidence packet functions as risk control and as strategy. Markets will favor organizations that adopt AI rapidly without hidden weaknesses. That edge will come from disciplined procurement, structured review, negotiating power with vendors, and the capacity to convert unclear systems into controlled work.

A company that maintains a strong packet can place sharper bets. It can accept a promising agent inside a defined workflow. It can decline a polished demo that conceals excessive agency. It can secure change-notice rights before a vendor updates the product. It can separate suppliers with genuine operational maturity from those that offer polished language but weak control records.

The distinction grows important because the AI market will continue to shift faster than standard contract cycles. Model costs may drop. Features may converge. Regulations may tighten. Incidents may alter insurance terms. Customers may question automated decisions. Employees may adopt tools without approval. A procurement record that captures evidence at the outset supplies institutional memory when conditions change.

The Practical Move

This week, take the next AI tool, agent, or workflow automation under consideration. Before scheduling another demo or pilot, request a two-page evidence packet:

  1. Page one from the vendor: material claims, limitations, data handling, security controls, change control, and incident response.
  2. Page two from the buyer: workflow, autonomy boundary, review owner, error budget, business proof, and stop rule.

Then decide. If both pages are clear, move to a bounded production pilot. If the vendor page is thin, narrow the scope or reopen terms. If the buyer page is thin, pause; the organization has not defined the task. If both pages are thin, the demo is theatre.

The organizations that succeed will not be those with the loosest procurement. They will be those whose procurement learns fast enough to approve with discipline and decline without alarm. The evidence packet is where that learning begins.
