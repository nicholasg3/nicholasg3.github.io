How to update on unverifiable coordination claims

False-flag / astroturf epistemics

Seed tweet · source: blog-shortlist #10

Coordination claims are recursive. The allegation that someone is running sockpuppets, false flags, or astroturf is itself a move in the fight. Communities that update too fast become tools for whoever can tell the cleanest villain story. Communities that refuse to update become numb, then blind, then surprised when receipts appear.

The right subject is not “believe every astroturf claim.” It is how to update under unverifiable coordination stories without becoming either credulous or nihilistic. AI politics makes this urgent: PACs, safety advocates, labs, and meme accounts all have incentives to narrate the other side as fake. Some of those narratives will be true. Many will be half-true. A few will be deliberate inversions.

The claim

You need separate confidence tracks for different objects:

  • Verified posts and actors — screenshots, archives, account histories you can open
  • Verified coordination — payments, vendor relationships, admissions, shared infrastructure, court-grade linkage
  • Pattern matches — timing, rhetoric, graph oddities, “this looks run”
  • Motive stories — “this helps someone, therefore they ran it”

People collapse these into one binary: “astroturf” or “not.” That collapse is how you get both witch hunts and willful blindness. Pattern match is not payment proof. Motive is not method. A real campaign can still include true claims. A true grassroots anger can still be amplified by paid infrastructure. The epistemology has to be multi-level or it will be weaponized.

Separate “this helps someone” from “this was run by someone.” Separate pattern matches from admissions. Write what actions are allowed at each confidence level before the next viral accusation arrives.

A concrete shape of the problem

Consider the public fight over pro-AI political money and AI-safety rhetoric. Investigations and reporting have, at various points, described sockpuppet or meme accounts tied to political infrastructure, extreme posts that parody or discredit a faction, and later blame-shifting about whose rhetoric causes harm. Details matter and should be checked against primary reporting. For this essay, the point is the shape, not a courtroom brief:

  1. Extreme or embarrassing content appears under a persona associated with faction A.
  2. Faction B says: this is who they really are.
  3. Faction A says: this is a false flag run by B.
  4. Journalists or investigators find some linkage — vendor, funding adjacency, admission, timeline — or they do not.
  5. Meanwhile, both sides use the unfinished story to freeze the other’s credibility.

If your community updates to certainty at step 2, you are a distribution arm. If you refuse to update at step 4 when primary linkage appears, you are doing identity maintenance. The skill is paced updating with explicit confidence tags.

Working rules

1. Tag claims by type. In any channel you control, force labels: observed content, alleged coordination, admitted or documented coordination, speculative motive. Mixing them in one sentence is how rumors become policy.

2. Prefer primary artifacts over moral summaries. “Deeply evil cartoon villain shit” may be emotionally accurate and still be a bad epistemic object. Link the investigation, the admission, the FEC-adjacent trail, the archive. Summaries without artifacts are for vibes, not for decisions.

3. Do not let “who benefits” finish the proof. Cui bono is a search heuristic. It is a terrible conviction standard. Almost every move in a polarized fight benefits someone. That is not the same as operational control.

4. Track admissions and vendor trails at higher weight than vibe graphs. When an organization admits paying for anonymous accounts, that is a different epistemic object than “these accounts feel coordinated.” Treat them differently in your notes.

5. Expect inverted astroturf as a method, not a meme. Actors can run ugly personas that look like their opponents, then point to the ugliness as proof the opponents are illegitimate. Actors can also falsely accuse real grassroots of being inverted astroturf. Both exist. Your process has to handle both without assuming either.

6. Cap action by confidence. At low confidence you may monitor and archive. At medium confidence you may warn your community about uncertainty. At high confidence you may name actors and change engagement rules. If every viral accusation immediately becomes a ban list, you have no epistemology — you have adrenaline.

A confidence ladder you can actually use

Level What you have What you may do What you may not do
L0 — Noise Screenshot, no archive; pure motive story Ignore or private note Public accusation; policy change
L1 — Content real Archived posts/accounts verified Quote content carefully; ask questions Claim coordination as fact
L2 — Pattern Timing, rhetoric, infra hints; still no payment proof Hypothesize; seek primary investigation Treat pattern as court-grade proof
L3 — Linkage Vendor, funding adjacency, shared ops, strong investigative case Raise confidence; brief stakeholders with caveats Collapse all related people into one villain
L4 — Admission / hard proof Admission, documents, or equivalent hard link Name the coordination; set engagement rules Invent extra crimes the evidence does not cover

Write your own version. The numbers matter less than the precommitment. Without a ladder, the loudest thread becomes your standard of proof.

What teams get wrong

Credulous speed. Safety-minded and acceleration-minded communities both do this. “This confirms what we already knew” is not a likelihood ratio.

Nihilistic freeze. After enough false alarms, people treat all coordination talk as schizo. That is how real influence operations get a free pass. Nihilism is not sophistication.

Identity-protective doubt. If the accused is “on our side,” standards of proof suddenly go to infinity. If the accused is outgroup, screenshots suffice. Invert your standards on purpose in review: what would convince you if the colors were swapped?

Policy by quote-tweet. Bans, hiring filters, donor decisions, and partnership freezes that track a 40-minute outrage cycle will regularly hit the wrong object. Slow the irreversible actions.

What to do

Write a public or team-internal standard before the next wave:

  • What evidence raises confidence in a coordination claim
  • What evidence lowers it
  • What actions are allowed at each level
  • Who is allowed to escalate from L2 to L3 in your org
  • How corrections are published when you overshot

If you research inverted astroturf dynamics yourself, keep methods analysis separate from unfinished case dumps. Methods travel; half-built dossiers become ammunition for people who do not share your carefulness.

For personal practice, keep a short log when a viral coordination claim hits your feed: claim type, best primary link, current level, action taken. That log will embarrass you into better calibration. Embarrassment is cheaper than becoming either a useful idiot or a permanent skeptic of everything.

The practical point

False-flag and astroturf claims are not a special domain where vibes replace standards. They are a domain where vibes are the attack surface. Update on content, pattern, linkage, and proof as different objects. Cap action by confidence. Prefer admissions and primary investigations over motive novels. Without that discipline, every viral accusation becomes policy by adrenaline — and the people best at manufacturing adrenaline write your epistemology for you.
