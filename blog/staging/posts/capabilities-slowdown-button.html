A slowdown button is not a control

Global capabilities slowdown as a coordination fantasy

Seed tweet · source: W26 / alignment politics

The claim

Wishing for a global capabilities slowdown is not a strategy unless there is a mechanism. When an insider says they would press a magic button if coordination existed, they are stating a preference conditional on an apparatus that does not exist. That can still be sincere. It is not yet a control.

Coordination fantasies are not empty. They reveal values: where people think the marginal month of capability work buys risk, and where they think safety research is starved. Opponents will say the button cannot exist without creating worse risks. Both sides need institutional objects, not wish-shaped metaphors.

The bind inside the wish

The interesting structure is not the opinion. It is the bind. A participant in the race voices a global pause while remaining embedded in the activity that defines the race. The wish is costless if the mechanism is impossible. “If we could coordinate” is a clause that can do legitimacy work without changing daily behavior.

There is a second bind. Some alignment work feeds on frontier capability. Mechanistic interpretability and related research often need the models they study. Slow capability and you may also slow the safety research that current models enable. Safety and capability are then entangled, not simply opposed. A naive pause can starve the mitigation capacity it hopes to protect.

The enforcement hazard

Accelerationist pushback has a real point even when the politics are loud. A button that truly works needs verification, sanctions, and shared measurement. That apparatus can become its own hazard: surveillance, capture, evasion races, and a centralized choke point that is more fragile than the open race it replaces. If the coordination mechanism is itself a catastrophic risk vector, the counterfactual “press the button” is incomplete until you describe the button’s attack surface.

Classic collective-action stories treat restraint as the social good and defection as selfish. Here the story is messier. Restraint may degrade co-produced safety capacity, and enforcement may raise new risks. Call that an entangled-brake problem, not a simple commons story.

What to do

Translate slowdown talk into enforceable objects. Name the gate: eval thresholds, compute reporting, export controls, liability rules, procurement bars, insurance conditions, lab-level deployment criteria. Say who measures, who punishes, who is allowed to defect under exception, and how defection is detected. If you cannot write the control, you are describing a mood.

Also test the entanglement claim instead of treating it as scripture. Ask whether the safety work you care about actually scales with frontier access, or whether it is mostly tooling, theory, and process that can run on lagged models. Without that distinction, “pause would hurt safety” is as unexamined as “pause always helps.”

Closing

Magic buttons are useful as stress tests of values. They are useless as policy. Prefer partial, ugly, real controls over pure counterfactuals that nobody can be held to. The adult version of the wish is a mechanism someone can audit, evade, or improve — not a button that only exists in the sentence that names it.
