Steal workflow structure, not mystique

Expert daily-driver LLM workflow

Seed tweet · source: W26 skill S=0.9

The claim

Practitioner daily-driver workflows are useful when distilled into checklists and verified beyond one person’s self-report. Steal structure, not mystique. Re-run the workflow on your tasks before you teach it as doctrine.

High-status demos travel poorly when curators flatten them into “just talk to it.” Plain language matters. So do context management, model choice, artifact discipline, and the quiet glance that rejects a bad result. The glance is often the expertise. It is also the part the highlight reel deletes.

How workflows get distorted

Expert material is often recut into democratization slogans. A technical walkthrough becomes “how he actually uses AI day to day.” A careful multi-step method becomes “the only skill is briefing.” Authority is imported from the practitioner, then used to dissolve the skill the practitioner is demonstrating.

That is useful for engagement. It is bad for transfer. Novices copy the surface moves and miss the judgment layer: what to put in context, when to switch models, how to keep files clean, when to stop trusting the thread. Replies that enumerate those techniques are not gatekeeping for sport. They are restoring the tacit layer the summary erased.

What is worth stealing

Steal the skeleton that survives translation to your stack:

  • How work is chunked into sessions
  • What always enters context first (repo map, tests, constraints)
  • Where outputs live as durable artifacts, not only chat
  • What checks run before a result is accepted
  • When the human must intervene

Ignore brand-name mystique, tool fetish, and claims that autonomy is one sentence away. Autonomy is a verification and accountability problem, not only a scheduling problem. If the workflow has no place for rejection, it is not a workflow. It is a vibe.

How to verify before you teach

Take the distilled checklist and run it on three of your real tasks: one easy, one messy, one high-stakes. Keep notes on where it breaks. Measure time-to-check, not only time-to-first-draft. If the checklist only works when you silently add your own judgment, write that judgment into the checklist or stop calling the method complete.

When you share a workflow, label what was observed, what was inferred, and what only worked for you. Self-report is a seed, not a standard. Multiple practitioners converging on the same structure is better evidence than one famous name plus a confident caption.

Closing

Daily-driver expertise is real and transferable when you extract structure and test it. It becomes harmful folklore when you steal the mystique and leave the checks behind. Copy the loop. Leave the aura.
