# Ground brief — stablecoins-monetary-sovereignty-sea

**Status:** grounded (2026-08-03)
**Vertical:** fintech
**Context:** publishes before Nick's 6 Aug NUS FinTech Lab podcast with Hassan Ahmed (Coinbase). Most sensitive of the three vertical pieces — reads closest to critical of a stablecoin issuer's product. Must stay measured, name the mechanism not a verdict, and steelman the pro-adoption case honestly.
**Claim (particular):** Cross-border payment efficiency and monetary sovereignty are the same phenomenon described from two ends. The event the stablecoin industry counts as a win — value enters a mostly-USD stablecoin and stays there because it is more useful than the local rail — is structurally identical to the event a central banker counts as a loss: currency substitution. Exposure is asymmetric across Southeast Asia: near-negligible for Singapore (freely convertible, well-managed SGD), sharper for economies with weaker or less convertible currencies, where the same mechanism resembles dollarization with a lower-friction interface. The revealed-preference welfare counterargument (M-PESA precedent) is real and must be given full weight, not strawmanned.

## Facts that must appear (with support)

1. **IMF's December 2025 Departmental Paper "Understanding Stablecoins" states stablecoins may contribute to currency substitution and capital-flow volatility, with risk concentrated in high-inflation, weaker-institution economies.** Total issuance exceeds $300 billion, about 7% of all crypto assets, roughly doubling over the prior two years.
   Supports: IMF Departmental Papers Volume 2025 Issue 009 (elibrary.imf.org and imf.org publication page), corroborated by Yahoo Finance and Coindesk coverage of the same paper.

2. **Aldasoro, Frost & Ito (BIS Papers No. 170) find approximately 98% of stablecoin value is USD-denominated** and set out three scenarios — niche/contained adoption, "digital dollarisation" that threatens EMDE monetary sovereignty, and domestic-stablecoin integration that preserves monetary control if regulatory infrastructure is strong enough.
   Supports: BIS Papers No. 170 (bis.org/publ/bppdf/bispap170.htm), also on SSRN.

3. **IMF Notes 26/01, "Tokenized Finance" (April 2026), argues tokenized settlement compresses the reaction time central banks have to intervene** — traditional T+2 settlement gives time to mobilize liquidity and net exposures before finality; 24/7 automated, algorithmic settlement removes that buffer. Stablecoins without central-bank reserve access are flagged as structurally similar to money market funds: stable until confidence breaks, then a run.
   Supports: IMF Notes No. 26/01 (imf.org/-/media/files/publications/imf-notes/2026/english/insea2026001.pdf), corroborated by The Block's reporting on the same note, quoting IMF economist Tobias Adrian.

4. **The "too small to matter" skeptic case is real and comes partly from inside the industry.** Noelle Acheson (Crypto Is Macro Now) and David Duong, Coinbase's own Head of Institutional Research, argue current stablecoin scale (~$300bn) is a fraction of US M2 (>$20 trillion), roughly 80% of stablecoin activity is crypto-trading collateral rather than treasury or remittance use, and that bond/equity flows would still dominate any macro move.
   Supports: CoinDesk, 10 Dec 2025 (coindesk.com/markets/2025/12/10/imf-flags-stablecoins-as-source-of-risk-to-emerging-markets-experts-say-we-aren-t-there-yet).

5. **The GENIUS Act (Section 4(a)(11)) bars US payment-stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders**, a deliberate line meant to keep payment stablecoins from competing with bank deposits as a savings product inside the US. It does not, and is not designed to, address extraterritorial substitution of a foreign country's own currency.
   Supports: CLS Blue Sky Blog (Columbia Law), 11 Dec 2025.

6. **The FSB's finalized "High-level Recommendations" (17 July 2023) is the global regulatory scaffolding for stablecoin oversight** — functional, proportionate, cross-border-coordinated regulation following a 2019 G20 mandate — the institutional backstop the piece references as the multilateral response track.
   Supports: FSB final report, 17 July 2023.

7. **Jack & Suri (2014, AER) is the empirical anchor for the revealed-preference welfare case.** Kenyan households using M-PESA saw no consumption drop after an income shock (0%) versus a 7% drop for non-users, driven by larger and more diversified remittances once transaction costs fell. Mobile-money adoption in their sample rose from 43% to 70%.
   Supports: American Economic Review 104(1): 183–223.

## Theory anchors used (4)

- **Brunnermeier, James & Landau, "The Digitalization of Money" (NBER Working Paper 26300 / BIS Working Paper 941, 2019).** Load-bearing frame: digital currencies can give rise to "digital currency areas" that form around platforms and networks rather than national borders, and can unbundle money's functions in ways that pull payments and savings away from domestic banks toward whichever platform is most useful — the mechanism this piece applies to stablecoins.
- **Calvo & Végh, "Currency Substitution in Developing Countries: An Introduction," IMF Working Paper 1992/040.** Classic vocabulary for currency substitution as a policy problem: once residents start using a foreign currency for transactions or savings, the process is self-reinforcing and hard to reverse by decree.
- **Ize & Levy-Yeyati, "Financial Dollarization," Journal of International Economics 59(2): 323–347 (2003).** Portfolio/hedging model showing dollarization is persistent once expected inflation volatility is high relative to real-exchange-rate volatility — supports the piece's claim that the effect concentrates in economies with a credibility problem, not spreads evenly.
- **Gopinath & Stein, "Banking, Trade, and the Making of a Dominant Currency," Quarterly Journal of Economics 136(2) (2021).** A currency's role as invoicing/unit-of-account currency is complementary to its role as a safe store of value, which is why substitution concentrates on one dominant currency (the dollar) rather than diversifying — ties directly to the verified 98% USD figure.

Considered and dropped: Rey, "Dilemma not Trilemma" (Jackson Hole 2013) — verified as real (NBER WP 21162) but its mechanism is capital-flow-driven monetary policy transmission via the global financial cycle, not currency substitution specifically; including it would stretch the piece's claim rather than sharpen it, so it was cut rather than force-fit.
