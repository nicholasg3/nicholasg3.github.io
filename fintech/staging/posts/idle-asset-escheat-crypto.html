Idle crypto meets escheat

Exchange custody does not freeze property law. After dormancy, states can take custody of unclaimed digital assets — sometimes only as cash proceeds.

CHEYENNE / REMOTE — Crypto users often treat an exchange balance as a permanent vault that waits until they return from years offline. U.S. state unclaimed-property law treats long silence differently. After a dormancy period without owner contact, holders of other people’s property — banks, brokers, now crypto platforms — must attempt notice, then report and deliver assets to state unclaimed-property offices. The doctrine is old. The asset class is new. The operational friction is real.

Coinbase’s public help center states the process in holder language. “Escheatment and unclaimed funds” explains that accounts may be deemed abandoned after inactivity under state law; that Coinbase monitors dormancy; that required notices go out by email or mail; and that if the owner does not reactivate, assets move to the unclaimed-property office of the relevant state. Once escheated, the assets are no longer held by Coinbase. Owners reclaim from the state, not by a casual support ticket that restores the old wallet balance in place.

Idle exchange balances are not outside property law. They are on a timer set by fifty states.

Native token or dollars

The same Coinbase page draws a product-critical fork. The company says it seeks to transfer eligible crypto in native form when state programs can accept it. Some states instead require conversion of some or all crypto to U.S. dollars before or after reporting. In those cases, the owner may be entitled only to the proceeds of that conversion when reclaiming from the state. Volatility between dormancy, liquidation, and claim payout is then a state process outcome, not an exchange “hack.” Cash balances transfer without that conversion step.

Public company risk factors match the help-center mechanics. Coinbase’s annual report language has long warned that the firm holds property subject to unclaimed-property laws; that foreign and U.S. jurisdictions may treat crypto unevenly; and that laws may require turnover after a specified period — including, in some formulations, airdropped or forked assets — and may require liquidation before remittance. That is disclosure of balance-sheet and customer-experience risk, not marketing copy.

How states wrote digital assets in

The 2016 Revised Uniform Unclaimed Property Act (RUUPA) included virtual currency in the definition of property, defining it roughly as a digital representation of value used as medium of exchange, unit of account, or store of value without U.S. legal-tender status. Uniform text did not settle every operational detail — dormancy length, due diligence form, remittance method — so states filled gaps. Practitioners track a wave of statutes and guidance that name digital assets explicitly and set reporting codes.

Arizona’s Department of Revenue, for example, publishes holder instructions for reporting digital assets, including virtual currencies, with a stated three-year dormancy period for that property type and directions that use NAUPA-style cryptocurrency codes. Other states set different dormancy clocks and different liquidation rules. The National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators points owners to multi-state search tools such as unclaimed.org — the same infrastructure used for forgotten bank accounts and insurance proceeds.

Why exchanges feel the teeth

Step What holders do Where crypto adds friction
Dormancy clock Track last owner-indicated interest (login, trade, update) HODL culture looks like abandonment under state tests
Due diligence notice Mail/email before remittance Stale addresses; users treat notices as phishing
Report (NAUPA formats) File holder reports with property codes Token identifiers and chains map poorly to fiat forms
Remit Deliver assets or cash to state Forced sale; loss of native exposure; tax events
Owner reclaim State verifies identity and pays/returns Months of lag; owner may receive only USD proceeds

None of this requires a novel theory of blockchain property. It requires treating exchange-held balances as ordinary intangible property under state statutes, with multi-state compliance programs that look more like a bank’s abandoned-property unit than a crypto support FAQ. Platforms that expand internationally inherit foreign unclaimed-property or dormancy regimes on top of the U.S. patchwork.

Product and custody design

Exchanges that want fewer surprises invest in dull controls: clear dormancy definitions in terms of service, current contact data, multi-channel notices that do not look like scams, and state-by-state matrices for native versus liquidated remittance. Self-custody removes the exchange as holder — and shifts loss modes to key management. For assets that remain on platforms, idle time is not free. It accrues legal process risk with balance-sheet and reputation consequences when large cohorts hit dormancy together.

Compliance teams feel escheat as calendar work. They maintain last-activity fields, state-of-address logic, notice templates, and remittance calendars that look like abandoned-property programs at brokerages. Product teams feel it as support load when users return to empty balances and angry screenshots. Finance teams feel it when large remittances move customer assets off the platform’s books and into state custody, with reconciliation and possible tax reporting consequences depending on jurisdiction and asset type.

Self-custody is not a free alternative for every user. It removes the exchange as holder and therefore removes that holder’s escheat duty — while introducing key-loss risk that no state unclaimed-property office will reverse. The institutional middle path is clearer custody agreements, regular owner-indicated activity that resets dormancy under applicable law, and honest product copy about what “inactive” means. HODL marketing that celebrates multi-year silence collides with statutes that treat multi-year silence as abandonment.

The strategic point is narrow. Crypto did not repeal escheat. It forced unclaimed-property offices and holders to invent codes, liquidation policies, and reclaim workflows for assets that can move globally in minutes and sit untouched for years. Users who vanish still have a path back through state portals. What they may not have is the same token, the same cost basis story, or the same timeline they imagined when they last closed the app.
