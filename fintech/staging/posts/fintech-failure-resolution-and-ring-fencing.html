Fintech failure without resolution is a political event

When client money, payment continuity, and group structure are not pre-planned for failure, insolvency becomes a supervisory and political crisis — not only a private wind-down.

FRANKFURT — Payments companies like to present as technology. In life they hold float, run merchant settlement, store customer credentials, and sit inside bank and scheme contracts. When that machine stops, ordinary corporate insolvency is a thin tool. Merchants want payouts. Customers want balances. Host regulators want local assets left in place. Home regulators want group control. Without ring-fencing of client funds and a resolution plan that someone can execute, the story leaves the bankruptcy court and enters parliament, the press, and the supervisor’s career risk.

After the global financial crisis, the G20 tasked the Financial Stability Board with a standard for resolving financial institutions that could be systemic in failure. That work produced the Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes for Financial Institutions, updated in 2014 and revised again in later FSB publications including a 2024 revised version. The Key Attributes aim at orderly resolution without severe systemic disruption and without exposing public funds to loss. Most mid-size fintech firms are not global systemically important banks. The standard still defines what “prepared failure” looks like — and how far ordinary tech-company insolvency sits from that bar.

If client money and critical payment functions are not separable on day one of stress, the state becomes a party to the failure whether it wants to or not.

What the Key Attributes demand that ordinary insolvency does not

The FSB framework assumes authorities have powers to maintain critical functions, impose losses on equity and eligible creditors, transfer businesses, and coordinate across borders. It assumes firms maintain information, operational continuity arrangements, and loss-absorbing capacity where systemic risk warrants it. Cross-border crisis management groups and cooperation agreements exist so home and host authorities do not surprise each other when a group fails.

Many fintech groups sit outside full bank resolution regimes. They may hold payment-institution or e-money licenses in one country, technology entities in another, and customer relationships that cross both. Client-money rules, if they exist, are statute- and license-specific. Resolution plans, if they exist, may be light recovery documents rather than operational playbooks with funding in resolution. When cash at a group company cannot be verified, or when settlement fails over a weekend, there is often no bridge institution tool and no pre-positioned loss-absorbing debt. There is only insolvency administration and political heat.

Wirecard: payments scale, ordinary insolvency

Wirecard AG made that gap concrete. On 25 June 2020 the company filed for insolvency after roughly €1.9 billion of reported assets could not be verified and were treated as missing. A European Parliament 2020 study on supervisory implications of the Wirecard case reconstructs the path: prolonged accounting questions, a KPMG special investigation that could not obtain reliable evidence for large parts of reported business, auditor refusal to sign the 2019 accounts, criminal complaints, and then insolvency of a firm that had sat in Germany’s blue-chip equity index.

Wirecard was not resolved under a bank-style Key Attributes playbook as a going-concern bridge with bail-in of pre-positioned total loss-absorbing capacity. It entered insolvency after trust in the cash line collapsed. The political aftermath ran through BaFin’s role, auditor oversight, short-seller investigations that had earlier targeted critics rather than the company, and years of civil and criminal process. Oxford’s Business Law Blog summarized a common diagnosis among corporate-governance scholars: internal controls, the supervisory board, external audit, reporting enforcement, and the market supervisor all failed in sequence. The lesson is structural. A large payments franchise without verified client and corporate cash segregation, without a credible public wind-down story, becomes a national scandal the moment the numbers stop balancing.

Why host authorities ring-fence

Ring-fencing has a precise meaning in cross-border resolution debates. Host authorities restrict transfers of local assets and liquidity to the parent or affiliates when they fear a group will drain the local entity to save somewhere else. The FSB’s single-point-of-entry strategies try to keep groups resolvable as wholes with downstream support. When that support is not credible — incomplete banking union, no fiscal backstop, weak home-host trust — hosts pre-position capital and liquidity locally or grab assets in stress. Fintech groups with thin local entities and centralized treasury feel the same pressure in miniature. A payments license in Market A and a technology parent in Market B is a ring-fencing invitation if client funds and settlement accounts are not clearly local and bankruptcy-remote.

Readiness element What “prepared” looks like What failure looks like in politics
Client money Segregated, reconcilable daily, bankruptcy-remote where law allows Merchants and users queue as unsecured creditors
Critical functions Mapped: authorization, settlement, dispute, custody Schemes and banks cut access without a transfer plan
Group structure Entities match licenses; intragroup funding documented Hosts freeze local cash; parents strand subsidiaries
Resolution / wind-down Playbook, data room, contact tree, buyer options Ad hoc insolvency plus parliamentary inquiry
Public perimeter Honest “payments firm” supervision, not tech theater Supervisor accused of capture or neglect

What banks and merchants actually check

Bank partners and large merchants already ask questions that look like miniature resolution reviews. Where is float held overnight. Which legal entity is the principal to the scheme. What happens to open authorizations if the processor’s parent files. How fast can settlement accounts be reconciled if the CEO is removed. Those questions are not theory. They are the difference between a contained administration and a political event that forces temporary public backstops, rushed asset sales, or emergency license transfers.

The FSB Key Attributes were written for banks, insurers, and financial market infrastructures that can be systemic in failure. Fintech platforms that clear material merchant volume or hold large customer balances inherit the same public expectation without always inheriting the same tools. Wirecard showed the cost of that mismatch. Orderly failure is a product feature. Without it, failure is a political event — and the brand that sold itself as software discovers it was always a financial institution in the eyes of the people who lost money.
