Open banking is a customer-ownership fight

Forced data portability does not abolish the bank account. It reopens who controls the interface, the advice layer, and the next product the customer buys.

WASHINGTON — For a decade, U.S. fintechs often reached bank data through screen scraping and bilateral contracts. That stack put technical risk on the consumer password and commercial leverage on the bank’s willingness to keep the pipe open. Section 1033 of the Consumer Financial Protection Act sat mostly dormant. On 22 October 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) finalized a rule meant to activate personal financial data rights as enforceable infrastructure.

The Required Rulemaking on Personal Financial Data Rights requires banks, credit unions, and other covered data providers to make consumers’ covered data available, upon request, to consumers and authorized third parties in a secure electronic form. It also defines obligations for third parties that receive that data, including limits on collection, use, and retention. The Bureau’s same-day press release framed the policy goal as competition and switching: consumers should be able to move data associated with bank accounts, credit cards, mobile wallets, and payment apps toward providers with better rates or service — without paying the incumbent for the handoff.

Portability reallocates distribution power. It does not settle who owns the customer.

Two layers of “ownership”

In ordinary speech, “owning the customer” mixes three different facts: who holds the legal account of record, who the consumer sees every day, and who earns the next sale. Open banking separates those facts on purpose. The data provider still runs the deposit or card relationship under banking and consumer-protection law. The authorized third party may run budgeting, lending offers, payment initiation, or switching tools with permissioned data. The consumer sits in between with a revocation right that both sides must honor if the rule works as designed.

That split is why banks and fintechs talk past each other. Banks hear forced free API access and rising fraud and liability risk on their rails. Fintechs hear a statutory right to compete on product without renegotiating scraping access every quarter. Both are describing the same multi-sided structure. Regulators are not awarding a mascot. They are rewriting who may touch the data exhaust of an account — and under which duties.

What the rule actually forces

The final rule is not a slogan about “open finance.” It is a developer-interface and authorization regime. Data providers must support reliable electronic access. Third parties must meet authorization conditions and privacy constraints. Industry standards bodies may gain a role in consensus technical standards under Bureau recognition paths described in the rule materials. The Bureau’s executive summary of the final rule restates the core duty: upon request, make covered data available to the consumer or an authorized third party.

Commercial consequences follow. Aggregators and app brands that previously lived on brittle credentials gain a compliance path — and new compliance costs. Large banks gain obligations to build and maintain interfaces at scale. Smaller institutions face fixed costs of access programs that may push them toward vendors. None of that is abstract. It shows up in roadmap budgets, vendor contracts, and who pays for fraud monitoring when a permissioned app misbehaves.

Customer ownership becomes a contract fight

Once portability is mandatory, the fight moves to fine print familiar from Europe’s PSD2 era and from U.S. bank–fintech partnerships: who is responsible when a data-powered payment goes wrong; how long a third party may retain data after a consumer revokes; whether the bank may still market its own products into the same session; how screen design steers the consumer toward or away from authorization. “Who owns the customer” becomes a stack of contracts and liability allocations, not a brand claim on a pitch deck.

Political risk is part of the product

Implementation calendars for large rules rarely survive first contact with courts and elections. The CFPB later opened a Personal Financial Data Rights reconsideration track, signaling that open-banking issues in the rule remain contested. Banks and fintechs that treated the October 2024 final rule as a fixed engineering deadline learned a familiar lesson: regulatory forced interoperability is a product environment, not a shipped standard. Teams still build interfaces and authorization flows. They also keep political and litigation scenarios on the same roadmap as API versioning.

European and UK open-banking programs already showed the industrial pattern: forced APIs create an aggregator industry, new consent UX, and multi-year fights over premium APIs versus free access. The U.S. rule is not a copy-paste of PSD2, but the commercial grammar is familiar. Whoever controls the authorization screen and the ongoing data refresh cadence influences which product the consumer sees next. That is distribution power with a legal wrapper.

The durable commercial fact is simpler than the litigation docket. Account data is a distribution asset. When law forces that asset to move at the consumer’s direction, fintechs can compete for attention and for the next product without owning the balance sheet. Banks can still win on trust, price, and the account of record — but not by locking the data exhaust as a private moat. Customer ownership becomes a measured split across ledger, interface, and liability, updated each time the consumer re-authorizes or revokes.
