Credit creation, not the fractional-reserve slogan

Public education still multiplies deposits by a reserve ratio. Central banks describe a different machine: loans create deposits, and capital, liquidity, and policy bind the volume.

LONDON — Explain banking at a dinner table and the fractional-reserve story still arrives first. Savers deposit cash. Banks keep a fraction in the vault. They lend the rest. That loan becomes another deposit somewhere. Money multiplies. The story is tidy. It is also a poor map of how most money appears in modern economies.

In March 2014, the Bank of England published “Money creation in the modern economy” by Michael McLeay, Amar Radia, and Ryland Thomas in its Quarterly Bulletin. The article states the core fact in plain English: most money takes the form of bank deposits, and the principal way those deposits are created is commercial banks making loans. When a bank grants a loan, it simultaneously credits the borrower’s deposit account. New money appears with the loan, not because a prior saver’s cash was physically handed across the counter.

Banks do not wait for a jar of deposits to “lend out.” Lending creates deposits — within constraints that are not the classroom reserve ratio.

Two misconceptions the BoE named

The Bank of England article rejects two popular pictures. First, banks are not pure intermediaries that only reallocate existing deposits from savers to borrowers. Second, they do not simply “multiply up” central bank money through a mechanical money multiplier driven by reserve requirements. In the modern sterling system the authors describe, reserves are used for settlement and for meeting regulatory liquidity needs; they are not the daily throttle that school diagrams imply. The quantity of broad money depends on banks’ willingness to lend and on borrowers’ demand, interacting with monetary policy — especially the policy rate — and with prudential rules.

That framing matters for fintech rhetoric. Founders still say “banks lend your deposits” as if deposits were pre-funded inventory. Marketplace lenders that do not take deposits sometimes claim they are more “real” because every loan is matched to an investor. Matching investors to loans can be a sound business. It is a different liability design from a bank that creates deposit money when it underwrites credit. Conflating the two confuses public education and product comparison.

When the reserve ratio went to zero

If the classroom model were the binding constraint, setting reserve requirements to zero would abolish limits on bank lending. U.S. experience shows otherwise. On 15 March 2020, the Federal Reserve announced that it would reduce reserve requirement ratios to zero percent effective 26 March 2020, in an ample-reserves operating framework (Fed press release). The Board’s reserve requirements page still records that all depository institutions face a zero ratio on net transaction accounts and other categories listed there.

The same March 2020 package stressed that banks had large capital and liquidity buffers and that supervisors expected those buffers to support lending. In other words, officials treated capital, liquidity coverage, funding markets, and risk appetite as the operative constraints — not a required reserve percentage. Zero required reserves changed a legal ratio. They did not repeal Basel-style capital rules, liquidity coverage ratios, stress testing, or the fact that bad loans destroy equity.

Why the slogan survives

The fractional-reserve slogan survives because it is easy to draw and because reserves once played a larger role in operating frameworks. It also flatters certain political stories: that banks “create money from nothing” with no limits, or that only a 100% reserve rule can restore honesty. The BoE account is less cinematic. Banks create deposit money when they lend, and they destroy it when loans are repaid. Limits are real, but they run through solvency, liquidity, regulation, and the policy rate — not through a simple vault fraction on a whiteboard.

For fintech operators, the clean distinction is liability design. A deposit-taking bank that extends credit is in the money-creation business as the BoE describes it, and lives under banking supervision. A non-bank lender funded by warehouse lines, ABS, or equity is in the credit business without creating bank deposits at origination in the same way. Both can fail. They fail under different run dynamics and different public backstops. Educating customers — and boards — with the 20th-century slogan makes those differences harder to price.

Journalists and crypto educators often reach for the multiplier when they want a villain or a magic trick. The BoE and Fed materials support a calmer sentence set. Banks create deposits with loans. They face real constraints. Those constraints changed when required reserves went to zero, but they did not vanish. Anyone building credit products — bank or non-bank — still needs a funding and capital story that survives a cycle, not a diagram of coins in a drawer.

The useful update is short enough for a product memo. Deposits are mostly bank liabilities created with loans. Reserve requirements in the United States are currently zero and are not the story of why credit expands or contracts. Capital, liquidity, funding, underwriting, and monetary policy are. Public education that still multiplies coins in a vault is teaching a museum exhibit, not the operating system of bank money.
