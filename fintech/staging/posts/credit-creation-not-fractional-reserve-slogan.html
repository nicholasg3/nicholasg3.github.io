Banks create credit. &quot;Fractional reserves&quot; is the wrong slogan.

Public debate still explains banking with a 20th-century reserve-ratio metaphor. Modern constraints are capital, liquidity, funding, and supervision. Teaching the wrong model produces the wrong fintech products.

The claim

A common internet explanation says banks lend out depositors' cash under a fractional reserve ratio. That story is incomplete for modern banking systems and actively misleading for product design.

In practice, loans create deposits subject to capital, liquidity, risk management, and funding constraints. Reserve requirements can be zero without making credit free.

Why the slogan persists

Fractional-reserve language is sticky because it feels mechanical and visual. It also fits political stories about "money printing by private banks."

Central bank educational materials — notably the Bank of England's money-creation explainers — have publicly corrected the simplified multiplier story for a general audience.

Fintech consequences

If you design neobank, lending, or stablecoin products on the slogan model, you will mis-price capital and liquidity. You will also misread why supervisors care about HQLA, NSFR, and credit risk models more than classroom reserve ratios.

This pairs with the zero-reserve-requirements memo: legal reserve ratios and credit constraints are different objects.

What to do

In founder education and investor memos, replace "fractional reserve" with the constraints you actually manage: capital ratios, liquidity coverage, deposit beta, and asset quality.

When someone cites reserve requirements as the binding limit on US bank credit, send them to Fed primary pages and capital rules, not a textbook cartoon.

Source notes

  • Bank of England — Money creation in the modern economy (Q1 2014): Canonical public explainer correcting simplistic money-multiplier teaching.
  • Federal Reserve — Reserve requirements: Primary page for US reserve requirement policy (including reduction to zero).
  • Basel Committee on Banking Supervision: Primary home for capital and liquidity standard framework.
