Agentic payments fail on authorization first

When software agents can spend, the scarce design is not model IQ. It is who may pay, for what, up to how much, and how the human revokes the key.

SAN FRANCISCO — Product demos of “AI that shops for you” still center on language models that browse, compare, and click. Payment networks are building a different layer. They treat the agent as a new initiator of card and account payments. The agent must prove authority the way a cardholder or a corporate travel-and-expense program already does — with limits, categories, authentication, tokens, and a kill switch.

Visa’s public framing is explicit. Visa Intelligent Commerce is a portfolio for agentic commerce: payment credentials, controls, authentication, and protections baked into automated buying so agents, issuers, merchants, and developers can transact inside defined trust boundaries. The network’s pitch is not that the model is smart. The pitch is that instructions and credentials load into rails that already know how to authorize, tokenize, and dispute.

Agentic commerce is a principal–agent problem with a checkout button. Authorization design is the product.

What “authorized” must mean

In classic e-commerce, the human is present at the click. In agentic flows, the human may be offline while software picks a merchant, an amount, and a shipping address. Merchants and issuers then need answers demos skip. Is this bot a permissioned buyer or an attacker? Which card token may it use? Is the amount inside a pre-set cap? Is the merchant category allowed? Does the purchase need a real-time human ping? Can the user revoke the agent without reissuing every credential in the household?

Those questions are familiar from corporate cards and virtual card numbers. Agentic payments recycle least-privilege design: grant the minimum power for the task, bind it to time and category, and log every use for dispute. Visa materials and partner coverage describe user-set dollar limits, merchant-category guardrails, and approval prompts that load into VisaNet-style authorization paths. Tokenization produces limited-use credentials rather than raw permanent card numbers.

Networks shipping the control plane

In December 2025, Visa said partners were completing secure AI-initiated transactions and that more than 100 partners were engaged, with sandbox and closed-beta activity among agent platforms (Visa investor news). Separately, Visa and Amazon Web Services announced Intelligent Commerce capabilities in AWS Marketplace and published blueprints for agentic retail and travel workflows. The materials emphasize authentication, agentic tokenization, personalization data, and user intent capture (AWS press release). The commercial message is consistent: developers should not invent payment authority from scratch inside a chat agent.

Other schemes are racing similar packaging. The strategic pattern matters more than any single brand. Card networks already intermediate trust between issuers and merchants. Agent platforms that want scale will route spend through rails that can answer liability questions when an agent over-orders, a prompt is hijacked, or a merchant ships goods the human did not want. Model quality without that rail is a support queue.

Where pilots still break

Authorization design fails in predictable places even when the demo succeeds.

Control What it is meant to stop How it fails in practice
Spend cap Runaway multi-purchase sessions Cap set per transaction while agent places many small orders
Merchant category Out-of-policy sellers MCC too coarse; marketplace sellers share a category
Step-up approval Surprise high-risk buys Approval fatigue; user auto-approves in the chat UI
Token lifecycle Credential theft and replay Long-lived agent tokens stored in logs or third-party memory
Revocation Former employee / shared household agent No single place to kill all delegated instructions

Dispute rights complicate the story further. Card networks evolved chargeback language for human-present and human-not-present card-not-present cases. Agent-initiated commerce sits between them. Until issuers, networks, and merchants share clear codes for “agent exceeded instruction” versus “user authorized the agent but regrets the purchase,” losses will land on whichever party has the weaker contract. That allocation is part of authorization design, not a later legal cleanup.

Autonomy is the wrong first milestone

Teams that lead with model autonomy often under-invest in the boring stack: instruction capture, cryptographic binding of agent identity, token minting, real-time policy checks, audit logs, and human revoke paths. Networks building Intelligent Commerce-style products are betting that those controls are what enterprises and issuers will buy. The agent that can reason about products is commodity. The agent that can spend inside a policy envelope that finance and fraud teams accept is the scarce good.

For issuers, the practical program looks like commercial card controls extended to software principals. For merchants, it looks like signals that distinguish good agents from scrapers — and refunds when the signal was wrong. For agent platforms, it looks like refusing to hold raw PANs and instead integrating network tokens with explicit instruction objects. Intelligence still matters for shopping quality. Settlement still fails first when nobody can prove the agent was allowed to pay.

Corporate travel and expense programs already prove the pattern at human scale: virtual cards, merchant locks, per-trip budgets, and manager approval. Agentic payments are that stack with a model choosing the itinerary. Organizations that already run policy engines for employees are closer to production than consumer chat apps that treat the card number as a secret pasted into a prompt. The consumer case will still matter for retail. It will inherit the same control vocabulary issuers already trust.

The near-term market will also sort standards. Some agent frameworks will speak network-native instruction objects. Others will invent private wallets that settle later. Until dispute codes and liability tables mature, finance teams will prefer agents that close the loop on Visa- or scheme-mediated authorization rather than opaque off-network rails. Model IQ will keep improving. Authority design decides whether that IQ is allowed to spend.
