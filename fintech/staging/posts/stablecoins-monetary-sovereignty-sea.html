The Cross-Border Efficiency Case for Stablecoins Is the Dollarization Case

The event a stablecoin issuer counts as adoption — money enters a dollar token and stays there because it works better than the local rail — is structurally the same event a central banker counts as currency substitution. The exposure is not the same everywhere in Southeast Asia.

SINGAPORE — When a Filipino overseas worker sends pesos home through a dollar stablecoin instead of a remittance operator, two things are true about the same transfer. The industry reads it as a win: a slow, costly, bank-hours-only channel just lost volume to one that clears in minutes, any day, for a fraction of the fee. A central banker in Manila reads the identical transfer as a loss condition: a household chose to hold and move value in a currency the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas does not issue and cannot expand or contract to manage the domestic cycle. Neither reading is wrong. They describe the same wire from opposite ends. The December 2025 IMF Departmental Paper "Understanding Stablecoins," by Tobias Adrian and thirteen co-authors, puts a name on the second reading: stablecoins "may contribute to currency substitution and increase capital flow volatility," with the risk "more pronounced in countries experiencing high inflation, weaker institutions, or diminished confidence in the domestic monetary framework." Total stablecoin issuance has roughly doubled in two years to more than $300 billion, the paper notes — about 7% of all crypto assets, and rising mostly on the back of dollar-denominated tokens moving through exactly the corridors where remittances, trade payments, and household savings already flow.

It is worth being precise about the mechanism before assigning it a verdict, because it cuts two ways and Southeast Asia does not face it evenly.

One mechanism, described from two directions

Markus Brunnermeier, Harold James, and Jean-Pierre Landau gave this mechanism its clearest theoretical frame before stablecoins existed at scale. Their working paper "The Digitalization of Money" (NBER Working Paper 26300, 2019) argues that digital currencies can give rise to "digital currency areas" — zones of monetary influence organized around platforms and networks rather than national borders. A currency area, in their telling, no longer needs geographic contiguity or a shared central bank; it needs a platform good enough that people route their transactions and savings through it regardless of where they live. Payments and, eventually, credit intermediation drift away from domestic banks toward whichever digital rail is most useful. Applied to stablecoins, the frame explains why "adoption" and "substitution" cannot be separated: a stablecoin becomes more useful precisely by doing the things a national currency is supposed to do — settle transactions, store value, price goods — and it does those things for a jurisdiction's residents whether or not that jurisdiction's central bank agrees to the transfer of function.

The older vocabulary for this is currency substitution, laid out by Guillermo Calvo and Carlos Végh in a 1992 IMF working paper that is still the standard reference: once residents begin using a foreign currency for transactions or savings, the process tends to be self-reinforcing and resistant to reversal by policy decree. Alain Ize and Eduardo Levy-Yeyati's 2003 model in the Journal of International Economics adds the savings side specifically: financial dollarization — the share of bank deposits and credit denominated in a foreign currency — persists once expected inflation volatility is high relative to real-exchange-rate volatility, and it persists even after a country stabilizes prices. That is the specific channel worth naming for stablecoins: money that would have sat in a local bank deposit, funding local credit, instead sits in a dollar token backed mostly by US Treasuries. The deposit base a domestic bank would have lent against does not disappear in the aggregate — it relocates to fund a different sovereign's borrowing.

Why it concentrates on the dollar, and where it concentrates in Southeast Asia

The scale of that relocation is not evenly spread across currencies, and the BIS's Iñaki Aldasoro, Jon Frost, and Hiro Ito put a number on the imbalance in a May 2026 paper: approximately 98% of stablecoin value in circulation is denominated in US dollars. Gita Gopinath and Jeremy Stein's dominant-currency framework explains why that concentration is close to inevitable rather than accidental. In their 2021 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper, a currency's role as the unit firms invoice in is complementary to its role as the currency safe assets are priced in — each reinforces the other, which is why substitution tends to converge on one dominant currency instead of diversifying across several. The dollar's position as the world's invoicing and safe-asset currency before stablecoins existed is the same position stablecoins now inherit and automate.

Aldasoro, Frost, and Ito lay out three scenarios for where this goes (see table below). Singapore sits closest to the contained end almost by construction: the Singapore dollar is freely convertible, MAS runs one of the more credible monetary frameworks in the region, and there is little reason for a Singaporean household to prefer a dollar stablecoin to SGD for domestic transactions or savings. The same is not obviously true for a saver in a country with recent history of double-digit inflation, capital controls, or a managed exchange rate under periodic pressure. For that saver, a dollar stablecoin is not a curiosity — it is dollarization with a phone number instead of a border crossing, available continuously rather than only through whatever channel capital controls were designed to police.

That speed matters on its own terms. An April 2026 IMF Notes paper, "Tokenized Finance," argues that traditional settlement — typically a two-day window — gives central banks time to mobilize liquidity, net exposures, and intervene before a shock becomes final. Tokenized, 24/7 settlement removes that buffer by design; automated margin calls and algorithmic execution compress the interval a central bank has to act. The same paper flags stablecoins without access to central-bank reserves as structurally similar to money market funds: calm and reliable until confidence breaks, at which point the run happens on the protocol's clock, not the regulator's.

Three scenarios, one uneven distribution of exposure

Scenario (Aldasoro, Frost & Ito, BIS 2026) What happens Who it concentrates on
Niche adoption Stablecoin use stays largely inside crypto trading; limited spillover to domestic money Most economies, most of the time to date
Digital dollarisation Dollar stablecoins substitute for local currency in everyday payments and savings Economies with weaker, less convertible, or less trusted currencies
Domestic stablecoin integration Regulated local-currency stablecoins or tokenized bank money capture the efficiency gain Economies with the regulatory infrastructure to build and supervise the alternative

The "too small to matter" case, and who is making it

The strongest skeptical case is not a strawman, and part of it comes from inside the industry that would benefit from the opposite conclusion. Noelle Acheson, who writes the Crypto Is Macro Now newsletter, and David Duong, Coinbase's own Head of Institutional Research, argued in coverage of the IMF paper that roughly $300 billion in stablecoins is a rounding error against US M2 of more than $20 trillion, and that roughly 80% of stablecoin activity is collateral for crypto trading rather than treasury management or remittance substitution. Duong's specific point is that stablecoins could plausibly accelerate a local flight to dollars at the margin, but remain small relative to the bond and equity flows that already dominate macro moves in most economies. That case deserves to be taken at face value rather than dismissed as industry spin: the numbers are real, and "risk exists in theory" is a different claim from "risk is material today."

The regulatory response so far treats the risk as real without treating the current scale as an emergency. The GENIUS Act, the US federal stablecoin law, includes a provision — Section 4(a)(11) — barring payment-stablecoin issuers from paying holders interest or yield "solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention" of the token. The rule is a US domestic design choice, meant to stop payment stablecoins from competing with insured bank deposits as a savings product inside the United States; it says nothing about, and was not built to address, a stablecoin substituting for a different country's currency. Internationally, the Financial Stability Board finalized ten high-level recommendations for global stablecoin arrangements in July 2023, covering regulatory authority, proportionate oversight, cross-border coordination, governance, and redemption rights — the multilateral scaffolding regulators are relying on while adoption is still closer to the first BIS scenario than the second.

The welfare case the mechanism does not answer

None of this settles whether substitution, where it happens, is bad for the people doing it. The strongest counterargument is not that the mechanism is wrong — it is that "loss of monetary sovereignty" describes an institution's interest, not necessarily a household's. William Jack and Tavneet Suri's 2014 American Economic Review paper on M-PESA is the empirical case that a low-friction transfer technology can be a straightforward welfare gain. In their Kenyan sample, households that adopted mobile money saw no measurable drop in consumption after an income shock, while non-user households absorbed a 7% decline; the difference came from larger, more diversified remittances once the cost of sending money fell. Mobile-money adoption in their data rose from 43% to 70% of households over the study period, driven by ordinary people making a rational choice about which channel served them better, not by a central bank's preference for which channel they should use.

A dollar stablecoin held by a saver in a country with unstable local currency is a version of the same choice. If the local currency is losing real value faster than a dollar token is losing usefulness, holding the token is not a defection from the national interest — it is a household managing risk with the best tool available, the same way Jack and Suri's Kenyan households used M-PESA to smooth a shock the formal banking system was not reaching. A central bank's loss condition and a household's rational choice can both be true descriptions of the same transfer, and the honest version of this piece's thesis has to hold both at once rather than resolve the tension in either direction.

What follows from that is a narrower claim than an argument for or against stablecoins: the mechanism is symmetric, and the policy question it raises is not "should this be stopped" but "which economies can afford to treat the underlying currency-quality problem as the one worth solving, and which cannot." Singapore's exposure to this question is close to academic. A saver in a country with a track record of currency instability is being asked, by the mechanism itself, to choose between a local currency an institution controls and a dollar token that works better — and the fact that the choice is now available around the clock, at low cost, without a bank branch, is exactly what makes it a monetary question and not only a payments one.
