SWIFT access is a political risk line

Threats to cut payment messaging access convert financial infrastructure into a geopolitical lever. In open economies, rail dependency is political risk as well as ops risk.

Seed tweet

BRUSSELS — SWIFT does not move balances the way a central-bank settlement system does. It moves messages that banks use to instruct payment. That distinction is easy to miss in headlines. It is also why disconnection hurts. When a bank loses the messaging network that counterparties expect, correspondent relationships and customer payments seize even if the bank still has a balance sheet.

SWIFT describes itself as a member-owned cooperative that replaced telex, connecting more than 11,000 banks, financial institutions, and corporations across more than 200 countries and territories, with headquarters in Belgium. On its sanctions FAQ, the cooperative stresses that it does not monitor or control the legitimacy of every user message. Sanctions compliance decisions rest with financial institutions and their authorities. The same institutional story includes a harder clause: when competent law requires disconnection, SWIFT complies.

Messaging access is operational plumbing until a legal order turns it into statecraft. Then it is a chokepoint.

The 2022 disconnects, in SWIFT’s own words

In its community update on Russia sanctions, SWIFT states that democratic decisions by the European Union, in consultation with the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States, brought the cooperative into efforts that required it to disconnect selected banks from financial messaging services. In compliance with EU Council Regulation (EU) 2022/345 of 1 March 2022, SWIFT says it disconnected seven designated Russian entities and their designated Russia-based subsidiaries on 12 March 2022.

The same update records further steps. Under EU Council Regulation (EU) 2022/398 of 9 March 2022, three Belarusian entities and their designated Belarus-based subsidiaries were disconnected on 20 March 2022. Additional EU regulations published on 3 June 2022 required further disconnections, which SWIFT says occurred on 14 June. The cooperative notes that it is incorporated under Belgian law and must comply with those regulations. The primary is SWIFT describing its own legal posture and dates.

The Iran precedent

The 2022 episode was not the first time messaging access became an instrument of sanctions policy. On 15 March 2012, SWIFT announced that, following an EU Council decision, it had been instructed to discontinue communications services to Iranian financial institutions subject to European sanctions. The 2012 press release is explicit about the instruction model: disconnection was framed as compliance with European legal requirements imposed on a Belgian cooperative.

That pattern matters for banks and firms outside the target states. It shows a repeatable mechanism. Authorities that can legislate for a network node can convert a technical membership question into a foreign-policy tool. Political scientists Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman call related dynamics weaponized interdependence: when global economic networks concentrate chokepoints, states that sit on those chokepoints gain coercive options. SWIFT is a classic candidate node because so many cross-border bank instructions assume it.

Sanctions rails are plural

SWIFT disconnection is only one instrument. On 24 February 2022 and in following packages, the U.S. Department of the Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control imposed expansive measures on major Russian financial institutions. In a Treasury press release, the department described actions against Sberbank and VTB among others, including correspondent and payable-through account restrictions designed to cut major parts of the Russian financial system off from U.S. dollar infrastructure. Treasury cited about $46 billion in daily foreign-exchange transactions by Russian financial institutions globally, with roughly 80 percent in dollars, as context for the disruption.

Those measures are legally distinct from an EU instruction to SWIFT. In practice they stack. A bank can lose messaging connectivity on one path and dollar correspondent access on another. For a corporate treasurer in a third country, the operational result looks similar: payment chains break, compliance filters widen, and banks reprice or exit corridors. “SWIFT” as a synonym for all financial sanctions flattens the stack. SWIFT as permanently neutral plumbing flattens the history.

What banks and firms in small open economies actually manage

Fintech firms and regional banks rarely set great-power sanctions policy. They do choose concentration. A product that assumes every counterparty bank will remain on the same messaging network forever underwrites a political constant as if it were uptime. Diversified settlement paths, clear sanctions screening ownership, and honest customer communication about corridor fragility are documented responses to a chokepoint that has already been used.

SWIFT’s own FAQ draws a boundary product teams keep in view. The cooperative says it does not decide the legitimacy of each commercial payment. Institutions and authorities do. At the same time, the 2012 and 2022 disconnection episodes show that membership itself can be withdrawn under law. Both statements can be true. The first is about message content. The second is about network access.

Payment messaging looks like neutral infrastructure when politics are quiet. When major powers legislate disconnection, the same infrastructure becomes a lever. Iran in 2012 and Russia and Belarus in 2022 are dated, primary events on SWIFT’s own channels. Firms that file rail dependency under “IT vendor risk” alone miss the line item that already moved history.
