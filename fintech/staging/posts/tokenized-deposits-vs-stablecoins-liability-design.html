Liability design separates tokenized deposits from stablecoins

Both can be tokens on a programmable ledger. One is usually a bank deposit claim that settles toward central bank money. The other is often a bearer instrument backed by a reserve portfolio. Chain choice is secondary.

BASEL — Product sheets blur the categories. “On-chain money.” “Tokenized cash.” “Digital dollars.” The marketing stack converges on speed, programmability, and 24x7 transfer. The balance-sheet stack does not. A tokenized deposit and a payment stablecoin can look similar in a wallet UI and still sit in different places in the hierarchy of money.

That difference decides who the holder has a claim on, what happens in a run, whether par exchange with other money is automatic, and which supervisors own the failure. Liability design is the product. The ledger is the messaging layer.

Liability design — not chain choice — separates tokenized deposits from stablecoins.

What Garratt and Shin made precise

In April 2023, Rodney Garratt and Hyun Song Shin published BIS Bulletin No. 73, Stablecoins versus tokenised deposits: implications for the singleness of money. Their key takeaways are blunt. Private tokenised monies that circulate as bearer instruments, like many stablecoins, may trade away from par relative to each other and to bank money, violating the “singleness of money” — the idea that different forms of the unit of account exchange one-for-one in ordinary payments. Tokenised deposits that do not circulate as free-floating bearer instruments, but instead settle in central bank money through the banking system, are more conducive to that singleness.

In both models, the authors note, private tokens can represent liabilities of an issuer with a claim to redeem at par in the sovereign unit of account. The transfer process differs. In the stablecoin-like model, the token moves as a digital bearer instrument; possession and transfer mechanics do more of the work. In the tokenised-deposit model, transfers update claims on regulated institutions and ultimately settle in central bank money the way commercial bank money already does. Programmability can attach to either design. Singleness does not automatically follow from a shared smart-contract language.

Why the distinction survives the next product cycle

The Bank for International Settlements returned to the theme in its 2025 Annual Economic Report chapter on the next-generation monetary system. In Chapter III, the BIS argues that stablecoins offer some promise for tokenisation use cases but fall short as the mainstay of the monetary system when tested against three attributes: singleness, elasticity, and integrity. Programmability, access, and pseudonymity features do not rescue a design that cannot reliably stay at par, expand with credit demand, or meet integrity standards expected of core money.

Tokenized deposits, when they remain deposits of supervised banks, inherit a different stack: prudential capital and liquidity rules, deposit insurance up to statutory limits in many jurisdictions, and access — for the bank — to central bank facilities that stabilize commercial bank money. The holder’s claim is on the bank. The bank’s settlement asset toward other banks is still central bank money. Stablecoin holders typically have a claim on an issuer (or a reserve structure) that is not the same as an insured deposit at a commercial bank, even when reserves sit in bank accounts or Treasuries. That is narrow-banking territory in economic function: money-like liabilities backed by safe assets, outside full credit-creating bank balance sheets.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s staff report “Stablecoins vs. Tokenized Deposits: The Narrow Banking Debate Revisited” (2026) models exactly that tradeoff: stablecoins backed by safe assets versus banks issuing deposits (traditional and tokenized) against mixed portfolios. Policy choice among allowing only tokenized deposits, only stablecoins, or both depends on regulatory cost and risk-shifting incentives. The paper’s existence is itself evidence that official-sector research treats the split as monetary architecture, not branding.

Design variants matter. The Financial Stability Board’s October 2024 note on tokenisation risks warns that if a “tokenised deposit” is structured as a transferable bearer-like claim that does not update the issuer’s books the way a deposit transfer should, it can inherit stablecoin-like singleness problems. Labeling is not liability. Supervisors will look through the token to the claim.

Product teams that treat “tokenized deposit versus stablecoin” as a pure technology choice mis-price capital, compliance, and redemption risk. Teams that start from the liability and work outward — who is the obligor, what settles the transfer, what happens at 3 a.m. on a stress day — pick a chain later, if at all. The ledger can be shared. The money hierarchy does not collapse because the interface is the same.

For banks, tokenized deposits are a way to offer programmability without ceding the deposit franchise to non-bank issuers. For stablecoin issuers, the product is portable digital cash with reserve transparency as the quality signal. Both can settle trades, pay invoices, or move collateral on a chain. Only one sits inside the two-tier banking system by default. Supervisors writing rules for “cryptoasset exposures” and “stablecoin arrangements” keep rediscovering that fork. Credit committees and product boards face the same fork. If two instruments share a wallet screen but not a liability, they do not share a risk model.
