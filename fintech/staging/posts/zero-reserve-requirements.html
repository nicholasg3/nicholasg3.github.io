Zero reserve requirements are not free money

U.S. required reserve ratios fell to zero in March 2020. That is a real legal change. Capital, liquidity, funding, and supervision still limit how much credit banks can book.

Seed tweet

WASHINGTON — A clean number travels well on social media. In March 2020 the Federal Reserve Board reduced reserve requirement ratios to zero percent. The Board’s own pages still say the change remains in force. For many readers the conclusion arrives in one step: the United States no longer has a fractional-reserve banking system, or banks can create credit without limit. The first claim overreads one rule. The second does not follow from the first.

Reserve requirements once forced depository institutions to hold vault cash or balances at a Federal Reserve Bank against certain deposits. When the ratio on those liabilities is zero, that particular legal obligation no longer binds. Capital rules, liquidity rules, market funding costs, deposit competition, and supervisory judgment still do. Confusing the zero ratio with free credit mixes a monetary-operations detail with the full constraint set of modern banking.

Zero required reserves changed a legal ratio. It did not abolish the limits that decide how much credit a bank can book.

What the Board actually did

On 15 March 2020, in a package titled Federal Reserve Actions to Support the Flow of Credit to Households and Businesses, the Board announced that reserve requirement ratios would fall to zero percent effective 26 March 2020, the start of the next reserve maintenance period. The same release linked the move to the Federal Open Market Committee’s shift toward an ample-reserves operating framework. In that framework, reserve requirements no longer play a central role in creating a stable demand for reserves.

The Board’s standing reserve requirements page states the outcome in plain language: the action “eliminated reserve requirements for all depository institutions.” The same page records the technical change in its historical table. Effective for the maintenance period beginning 26 March 2020, the 10 percent ratio on net transaction deposits above the low reserve tranche and the 3 percent ratio inside that tranche both fell to zero. The Board estimated that required reserves fell by about $200 billion.

That estimate matters for scale. A $200 billion reduction in required balances is large as a regulatory relief line. It is still a change in one instrument inside a system that already ran with abundant reserves after years of large-scale asset purchases. The press release did not claim that banks were freed from capital rules, stress testing, or liquidity regulation. It described a monetary-operations choice in a crisis week, alongside other credit-support measures announced the same day.

What still constrains credit

Modern bank lending is multi-constraint. Economists who treat banks as credit creators rather than pure loanable-fund intermediaries are not describing a world without limits. They are describing a different mechanism than the textbook money-multiplier cartoon that still shapes public argument. In the cartoon, required reserves are the throttle. In practice, risk-weighted capital, leverage ratios, internal risk appetite, and the cost and stickiness of funding often bind first.

Liquidity rules illustrate the point. In September 2014 the federal banking agencies finalized the Liquidity Coverage Ratio rule for large banking organizations. Covered firms must hold high-quality liquid assets sufficient to cover projected net cash outflows over a 30-day stress period. The ratio applies on a stringent basis to organizations at or above specified size and foreign-exposure thresholds, with a modified version for smaller covered holding companies. Whatever one thinks of the calibration, LCR is a post-crisis liquidity constraint that does not disappear when Regulation D reserve ratios are zero.

Capital is a second ledger. Supervisory capital requirements, buffers, and stress-test outcomes limit how much risk-weighted exposure a bank can carry for a given capital base. A bank that is capital-constrained cannot “print” its way around a shortfall by pointing to zero required reserves. Depositors and wholesale funders also price risk. When funding costs rise or deposits run, balance-sheet capacity shrinks even if the legal reserve ratio is zero.

Why the slogan travels

Zero is a complete sentence. “No reserve requirements” sounds like a hidden switch. In high-attention markets, a true but partial fact outruns multi-rule reality. Crypto and fintech audiences often learn banking through the money-multiplier story first. When that story’s throttle is set to zero, the narrative wants a regime change. The Fed’s own words point somewhere narrower: an ample-reserves implementation choice that removed a tool already sidelined for policy control.

Banks and fintech firms that design deposit-like products or bank partnerships feel the cost of the slogan. Customers who believe banks face no constraints misread stress events, capital calls, and product risk disclosures. Founders who believe the same may underwrite credit as if only marketing and demand matter. Both errors surface when the next funding shock arrives and the residual constraint set reasserts itself in public.

Reading the residual stack

Risk memos that stay useful separate lines the March 2020 action changed from lines it did not. One line records reserve requirement status: ratios at zero under current Board policy. A second line records capital and stress outcomes for the relevant charter. A third records liquidity metrics such as LCR where applicable. A fourth records deposit concentration, wholesale dependence, and run dynamics. Collapsing those lines into “fractional reserve is over” produces a story that fails as soon as any non-reserve constraint binds.

Primary sources help because they are short. The 15 March 2020 press release states the effective date and the ample-reserves rationale. The reserve requirements page states that requirements were eliminated for all depository institutions and quantifies the estimated drop in required reserves. Neither document announces unlimited credit. They announce a ratio of zero on a rule that had already lost its central role in monetary-policy implementation.

U.S. banks do operate with zero required reserve ratios. That fact is public, dated, and primary. Credit still faces capital, liquidity, funding, and supervision. The public debate that treats the zero ratio as free money is arguing with a cartoon throttle while the real switches sit on other panels.
