Zero reserve requirements are not free money

US required reserve ratios fell to zero in March 2020. That is a real legal change. It is not the same thing as unconstrained credit creation.

Seed tweet

The claim

A recurring social post says the United States no longer has a fractional-reserve banking system because reserve requirements are zero. The Federal Reserve did reduce reserve requirement ratios to zero percent, effective March 26, 2020. That fact is public and primary.

The jump people make next is the problem. Zero required reserves is not the same as unlimited lending, and it is not the same as the end of banking constraints. Capital rules, liquidity rules, deposit funding, supervision, and internal risk limits still bind.

What the Fed actually did

On March 15, 2020, the Board announced it would reduce reserve requirement ratios to zero percent. The Board’s own reserve-requirements page still states that the action eliminated reserve requirements for all depository institutions and remains in effect.

Historically, reserve requirements forced banks to hold vault cash or balances at the Fed against certain deposits. Cutting the ratio to zero removed that particular binding constraint. It was a crisis-era monetary-operations choice, not a repeal of bank regulation as a whole.

What still constrains credit

Modern banks are constrained by capital ratios, liquidity coverage, net stable funding, stress testing, and supervisory judgment. They are also constrained by deposit competition and market funding costs. Thread replies under the seed post correctly noted that other regulations limit credit creation even when reserve ratios are zero.

Economists who describe banks as credit creators rather than pure intermediaries are not describing a world without limits. They are describing a different mechanism than the textbook money-multiplier cartoon. The seed post’s useful provocation is the cartoon; the operator lesson is the residual constraint set.

Why the slogan travels

Zero is a clean number. “No reserve requirements” sounds like a secret. In a high-attention environment, a true but partial fact outruns the multi-rule reality of bank funding.

For fintech operators, the lesson is not “banks can print without limit.” It is: public financial literacy still runs on obsolete metaphors, and products that depend on public trust will collide with those metaphors during every crisis narrative.

What to do

If you build or regulate deposit-like products, write three separate lines in your risk memo: reserve requirement status, capital/liquidity constraints, and supervisory perimeter. Do not let a viral banking slogan collapse those lines into one.

If you communicate with users about “how banks work,” prefer primary Fed language over crypto-twitter simplifications. The primary source is short and clearer than most threads.
