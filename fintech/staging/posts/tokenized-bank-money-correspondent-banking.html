Tokenization Speeds Settlement. It Does Not Replace Correspondent Banking.

Money stays a liability of whoever issued it. Moving value between issuers still needs a bilateral deal, a market maker, or the bank system underneath — a faster ledger changes none of that.

BASEL — On 30 July 2026, the Bank for International Settlements confirmed that Project Agorá had moved real money across borders for the first time: roughly CHF 800,000, split across six currencies, settling around 30 transactions in about 80 seconds each. Twenty-eight commercial banks and five central banks took part. The headline is speed. The detail that matters more is who carried the payment: the same commercial banks that would have carried it through the correspondent-banking chain Agorá was built to test against. Tokenization compressed the messaging, the compliance checks, and the settlement step into one ledger update. It did not remove the banks, because the banks were never a messaging inefficiency. They were the trust.

That distinction is the whole argument. Money is not a neutral technical object that becomes interchangeable once it is represented on a faster rail. It is a liability of whoever issued it — a Citi dollar, a JPMorgan dollar, a Circle dollar, a Singapore dollar deposit at DBS. Moving value from one issuer's books to another's has only ever had three routes: a bilateral acceptance arrangement between the two issuers, which is a nostro/vostro relationship regardless of what the ledger underneath looks like; an exchange transaction where a market maker takes the spread for bridging the gap; or redemption back through the banking system that backs the token in the first place. Tokenization can make each of those three routes faster and more transparent. It cannot make them disappear, because the underlying problem — one party has to trust another party's liability — is not a settlement-speed problem.

A hundred dollars that never crosses a border

The Bank for International Settlements made this point directly in Chapter III of its 2025 Annual Economic Report, using a plain example: a US manufacturer pays a Korean supplier $100. Under the correspondent-banking system, no dollar physically travels to Korea. The payer's bank debits an account in the United States, a correspondent bank credit moves through accounts held in each jurisdiction, a foreign-exchange conversion happens somewhere in the chain, and the two domestic settlement legs are coordinated through repeated messages between the institutions involved. The report's language is exact: "No money moves across borders; settlement in central bank reserves occurs entirely within the United States." Tokenizing that same payment does not change the description. It still requires two domestic settlement legs, still requires an entity willing to bridge the currencies, and still needs "all private tokenised money issuers [to] comply with the same regulatory standards" for the tokenized version to trade at par with cash, in the report's own words. The ledger gets faster. The two-tier structure — central bank money underneath, commercial bank liabilities on top — stays exactly where it was.

The IMF's April 2026 Fintech Note, "Tokenized Finance," makes the same argument from a different angle: tokenization is a structural shift in financial architecture, not a marginal efficiency upgrade, and its payoff depends on anchoring digital finance in public trust, safe settlement assets, and legal certainty across jurisdictions. That is a polite way of saying the hard part was never the software.

Three live answers, one shared admission

Three architectures are competing to solve fragmented issuer liability right now, and each one, by its own design, concedes that tokenization alone does not do the job.

Architecture How it solves the trust problem Live example
Consortium coin Competing issuers merge into one shared issuer and one governance structure OUSD / Open Standard, announced June 2026
Dominant single coin One issuer becomes large enough that everyone else's liability converts through it by default Existing dollar-stablecoin concentration (Tether, Circle)
Bridging / interoperability layer A shared ledger and set of intermediating institutions coordinate settlement across issuers who keep their own liabilities Project Agorá, BIS-led, real-value test July 2026

A consortium coin resolves fragmentation by corporate merger, not by protocol innovation. A dominant single coin resolves it by market concentration, the same way a reserve currency resolves it among sovereign currencies. A bridging layer resolves it by keeping the intermediating institutions and speeding up the coordination between them, which is precisely what Agorá tested. None of the three routes removes the need for someone, somewhere, to accept somebody else's liability at par. They only change who that someone is.

OUSD merges the trust instead of automating it

The clearest recent case of the consortium route is Open USD. On 30 June 2026, more than 140 companies that compete directly with one another — Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Stripe, BlackRock, Coinbase, Google, and BNY among them — agreed to back a single stablecoin issued through a new, deliberately independent entity called Open Standard, with Bridge co-founder Zach Abrams as interim chief executive. Economist Christian Catalini, who worked on Meta's Libra project, wrote in Forbes the same day that OUSD "revives Libra's vision for a unified, open protocol" and argued the design wins not because of better cryptography but because competing firms "agree to a standard" rather than run parallel, fragmented tokens. His own account of why it works is a governance story: OUSD charges nothing to mint or redeem, and returns reserve income to the distributing companies rather than to the issuer, which is what got 140 rivals to sign the same document. Nothing in that account describes a technical breakthrough in how value moves. It describes a merger of trust among issuers who had previously refused to trust each other's tokens.

Regulators are writing the same limit into law

US and Singaporean regulators have both encoded this constraint rather than assumed it away. The GENIUS Act, the federal stablecoin framework moving through Congress in 2025-26, does not presume that competing US-regulated stablecoin issuers will interoperate once regulated. Section 12 instead requires federal regulators to first assess whether technical compatibility standards between issuers are even necessary, in consultation with the National Institute of Standards and Technology, before they may prescribe any. Interoperability, in other words, is treated as a policy decision regulators might have to force, not a property tokenized dollars acquire automatically.

The Monetary Authority of Singapore's BLOOM initiative, launched in October 2025, is built around the same admission. Its named workstreams split "distribution and clearing of settlement assets" from "programmable controls" that enforce compliance checks, with participants including Circle, Coinbase, DBS, OCBC, UOB, Partior, Stripe, Ant International, and StraitsX assigned across those two tracks. A regulator convening its own multi-currency settlement initiative still organizes the work around who clears for whom and under what check — not around a single token format that would make the question moot.

What free banking already taught

Gary Gorton and Jeffery Zhang's "Taming Wildcat Stablecoins," published in the University of Chicago Law Review in 2023, is the theory anchor that fits this pattern best. Their argument is that stablecoins structurally resemble the banknotes issued by thousands of individual US banks before the Civil War: privately issued money that did not automatically trade at par outside its issuer's home territory, and whose acceptance depended on clearinghouse arrangements or, eventually, a central bank backstop — not on any improvement to the paper itself. Gorton and Pennacchi's 1990 work on financial intermediaries makes the underlying mechanism explicit: debt is valuable as money specifically because it is "informationally insensitive," meaning a counterparty does not have to investigate the issuer before accepting it. That insensitivity is not a property of the ledger. It is manufactured by regulation, deposit insurance, or a shared backstop, and every issuer that wants its liability to travel has to either build that trust itself or borrow it from someone who already has it. Kahn and Roberds' 2009 survey of payments economics frames the same conclusion at a higher level: payment systems are joint problems of credit risk, finality, and information, not pure engineering problems, which is why a faster rail changes the second variable without touching the first.

Put the three current architectures next to that theory and the pattern holds. A consortium coin recreates the clearinghouse by having issuers merge outright. A dominant coin recreates the reserve-currency effect by out-growing everyone else's need for a clearinghouse at all. A bridging layer like Agorá recreates correspondent banking with better messaging and calls it a unified ledger. Settlement in each case gets faster, sometimes dramatically — 80 seconds instead of the multi-day chains correspondent banking still runs on for retail cross-border payments. The topology of who has to trust whom to accept a promise at par has not moved. Twenty-eight banks still stood in the middle of Agorá's July transaction for the same reason 28 banks would have stood in the middle of it in 1995: somebody has to vouch for the liability crossing the border, and tokenizing the ledger does not tell you who that should be.

Reader Decision Ask which of the three trust routes a token uses, not how fast it settles.

Consortium merger, market-maker spread, or bank redemption — every cross-issuer transfer still runs through one of the three. Settlement speed does not change which one applies.

Market Signal OUSD and Agorá are the same admission from opposite directions.

OUSD merges issuers into one. Agorá keeps issuers separate and speeds up the banks coordinating between them. Neither removes the coordination.
