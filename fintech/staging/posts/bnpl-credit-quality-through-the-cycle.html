BNPL looks free until the cycle turns

Buy-now-pay-later products market smoothness at checkout. Credit quality, late fees, and household leverage show up later. BNPL is consumer credit through the cycle, not a payments feature.

WASHINGTON — At the payment screen, buy-now-pay-later often looks like a button, not a loan. Four installments. Zero interest if the borrower pays on time. Merchants see higher conversion. Lenders see take rates and gross merchandise volume. Households see a smaller number due today.

Underwriting still happens. Someone is extending credit on a purchase, collecting installments, charging late fees when payments slip, and absorbing losses when they do not. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s market work treats that stack as consumer credit with a checkout wrapper. Investors who model only approval rates and GMV miss charge-offs, servicing cost, and funding sensitivity when labor markets soften.

BNPL is consumer credit with a checkout UX. Cycle risk is the product risk.

What the CFPB measured in the growth years

In September 2022 the Bureau published Buy Now, Pay Later: Market trends and consumer impacts, drawing on market-monitoring orders to major BNPL firms and public filings. The Bureau’s study summary reported that loan approval rates rose to 73 percent of applicants in 2021, from 69 percent in 2020. Late fees became more common: 10.5 percent of unique users were charged at least one late fee in 2021, up from 7.8 percent in 2020. Returns also rose: 13.7 percent of individual loans in 2021 had at least some portion of the order returned, up from 12.2 percent in 2020.

The same report flagged three risk buckets that still organize the product: discrete consumer harms (including thin disclosures and dispute friction), data use that can push incremental sales, and borrower overextension. On credit performance, the report described deteriorating BNPL credit conditions into 2022. It cited a U.S.-based BNPL lender whose dollar charge-off rate (net charge-offs as a percent of GMV) rose from 1.39 percent in FY 2020 to 2.17 percent in H1 2021, 2.31 percent in H2 2021, and 3.03 percent in H1 2022. An Australia-based lender with most volume in the Americas reported a similar climb toward about 3 percent in FY 2022 from about 1.6 percent the prior year. Those paths are not a full industry loss curve. They are enough to show that BNPL losses move when underwriting loosens and the cycle turns.

Stacking, scores, and the invisible balance

A later Bureau study filled a different gap. Many BNPL loans have not been fully visible on traditional credit files, so outside underwriters could not see how many pay-in-four lines a household already carried. In January 2025 research summarized by the CFPB newsroom and detailed in Consumer Use of Buy Now, Pay Later and Other Unsecured Debt, the Bureau matched pay-in-four originations from six large firms — Affirm, Afterpay, Klarna, PayPal, Sezzle, and Zip — to de-identified credit records.

Among consumers with a credit record, 21.2 percent financed at least one purchase with BNPL in 2022, up from 17.6 percent in 2021. About 20 percent of 2022 borrowers were heavy users who originated more than one BNPL loan on average each month. Average originations per borrower rose from 8.5 to 9.5. Sixty-three percent of borrowers originated multiple simultaneous loans at some point in the year, and 33 percent took loans from multiple BNPL lenders. Nearly two-thirds of loans went to borrowers with subprime or deep subprime scores; among those applicants, lenders approved 78 percent of loans in 2022. BNPL borrowers were also more likely than other consumers to carry higher balances on other unsecured lines such as credit cards.

That is stacking in plain language. A single merchant’s conversion metric can look clean while the household’s total installment load rises across apps. When BNPL is under-reported to credit bureaus, card issuers and mortgage underwriters can understate leverage. Through-the-cycle credit work has to assume that opacity rather than hope for clean files.

Payments economics versus credit economics

BNPL sits across two ledgers that diligence memos should not mix. Payment processing earns fees for authorizing and settling a purchase. Credit earns net interest or merchant discounts while bearing expected loss, cost of funds, and collections. Zero-interest pay-in-four often shifts the explicit finance charge into merchant discount and late fees. The household still faces a payment schedule. The lender still faces a vintage.

Metric What it often tracks Where it misleads
Take rate / GMV Merchant adoption and top-line volume Ignores charge-offs and servicing cost
Approval rate Underwriting tightness at a point in time Rises into easy labor markets, then reverses
Late-fee incidence Payment stress at the edge Fee income can mask rising risk cohorts
Simultaneous loans per borrower True leverage and stacking Invisible when BNPL is off bureau files
Charge-offs % of GMV Credit outcome through the cycle Lagged; growth cohorts look fine until they age

Merchant concentration adds correlation. If a BNPL book leans on apparel, travel, or a handful of large retail partners, a segment slowdown hits both originations and recoveries. Funding models that depend on warehouse lines or capital-markets appetite reprice when credit spreads widen. Growth cohorts underwritten when unemployment is low are not a strategy. They are a vintage.

Regulators have spent several years treating BNPL as credit that needs clearer disclosures, fair collection practices, and better visibility into household debt. Markets that still price BNPL as a pure payments attach will relearn the difference when delinquencies rise together. The button at checkout can stay soft. The underwriting still has to survive a harder labor market.

Partnership design follows the same split. A retailer that embeds BNPL to lift conversion is distributing someone else’s credit book. Information rights on losses, late fees, and complaint themes are part of the commercial deal, not a courtesy. When the cycle turns, the merchant still wants the conversion button. The lender wants tighter cutoffs. Contracts that only priced GMV share leave both sides negotiating under stress with incomplete data. Through-the-cycle discipline is operational before it is rhetorical.
