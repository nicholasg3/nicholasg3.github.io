The remittance fee is not the full price

Advertised transfer fees can hide FX margin, receiving-side charges, and float. Unit economics sit in corridor control and total price, not in a single fee tile.

A migrant sending money home often sees one number in bold: the fee. That number is easy to compare. The amount that arrives can still disappoint. Foreign-exchange margin, charges taken at the receiving end, slower value dating, and limited cash-out points all move the economics without changing the bold fee. The industry has known this structure for years. Public policy instruments were written to force the full price into the open.

In January 2007 the Committee on Payment and Settlement Systems (now the CPMI) and the World Bank published General Principles for International Remittance Services. The report defines remittance transfers, for its purposes, as cross-border person-to-person payments of relatively low value — typically recurrent payments by migrant workers. It is blunt about market failures: lack of transparency, weak infrastructure, legal friction, thin competition, and risk. Its first general principle puts transparency and consumer protection at the center. Providers should make the speed and the total price of the service clear, including fees that may be charged to the receiver.

If the fee falls and the exchange rate worsens by more, the sender did not get a cheaper remittance. The provider moved the margin.

Why total price is the only honest unit

The CPSS/World Bank analysis separates service types in a way that still maps to product screens. Some services are “closed”: the provider controls both ends and can state a total price and a delivery time with confidence. Other bank-based services are more “open,” routing through correspondent chains toward almost any receiving bank. That global reach is valuable for thin corridors. It also means the sending institution may not know the fee the receiving bank will take or the total time funds will take to arrive. A low send fee in that model can coexist with an opaque all-in cost.

Foreign exchange is the usual hiding place. A provider can advertise a zero or near-zero transfer fee and still earn a wide spread between the interbank rate and the rate given to the customer. Float is another. Holding funds an extra day is an interest-earning or working-capital choice for the provider and a liquidity cost for the household. Exclusive cash-out networks and agent commissions add still more layers. None of those lines must appear in the bold fee tile.

The World Bank’s Remittance Prices Worldwide project exists because corridor-level measurement is the only way to compare providers on a common basis. RPW publishes price information across corridors so policymakers and the public can see more than marketing screenshots. The exact quarterly global average moves over time and should be read from the latest RPW release rather than from memory. The institutional point is stable: price transparency is a public measurement problem, not only a brand promise.

Competition is the intended lever

The 2007 report’s strategy is not price caps as a first principle. It argues that the best path to lower prices and better access is a more contestable market — open to a wide range of remittance service providers on a level playing field, with sound legal underpinnings and enough regulation to correct market failures. That framing still challenges two popular fintech shortcuts.

The first shortcut is fee-only competition. A new app that undercuts the fee while monetizing FX and float has not rewritten corridor economics. It has relocated the spread. The second shortcut is infrastructure romance. Faster messaging or a blockchain rail can cut some intermediation, yet cash-out density, compliance cost, foreign-exchange inventory, and agent networks still dominate many household corridors. When those remain concentrated, the surplus often stays with the party that controls last-mile payout or dollar inventory.

What firms and policymakers can measure

Product teams that want a real cost advantage need metrics that match the general principles. All-in cost for a fixed send amount, at a stated speed, with a disclosed receive amount in local currency, is the comparison that matters. Split testing a fee tile without locking the FX rate is a conversion trick, not a welfare improvement. Corridor-level unit economics should show where gross margin comes from: fee, FX, float, or agent markup.

Policymakers already have the outline. Transparency rules that force total price disclosure, open access to payment infrastructure on fair terms, and competition among remittance service providers are the CPSS/World Bank toolkit. Development targets that aim to reduce remittance costs sit on top of that plumbing. None of those tools work if public debate only tracks the advertised fee race.

Reading fintech claims

Every few years a new rail promises to collapse remittance cost to near zero. Sometimes the operational cost of messaging does fall. Households still pay for compliance, fraud, capital, cash distribution, and currency risk. Providers still choose how much of that cost to show as a fee and how much to embed in the rate. The durable question is who captures the corridor surplus after the press release.

The 2007 general principles remain useful because they refuse the fee-only frame. They define the market, name opacity as a core failure, and treat contestable structure as the main price lever. RPW exists to keep scores. Firms that compete on total price and corridor reach are playing the game the public instruments describe. Firms that compete on fee tiles alone are playing a screen.
