Cheap capital can fake efficient rails

Many fintech products look efficient while customer acquisition, rewards, and float are venture-subsidized. When funding costs rise, the rail did not get worse — the subsidy ended. Unit economics must separate transfer cost from growth spend.

The claim

Investor narratives often credit technology for fee compression in payments, brokerage, and neobanking. Technology matters. So does the price of capital used to buy users and waive fees.

If contribution margin is negative after fair payment processing costs, you are not looking at a mature rail. You are looking at a growth campaign with a payments skin.

What to separate in the model

Variable cost to move money or extend credit. Rewards and fee waivers. CAC payback. Cost of capital. Credit losses through the cycle.

Public company filings and investor presentations sometimes make these lines visible. Seed-stage decks often blend them into a single "take rate" story.

Why this matters now

When rates and risk appetite change, subsidized products either raise prices, cut rewards, or die. Competitors who underwrote real unit economics look "suddenly" better without inventing new rails.

Regulators also care when deposit-like products grow on incentives that reverse quickly.

What to do

In diligence, recompute unit economics with rewards and float benefits set to zero. If the product disappears, you found the real engine.

In product strategy, track subsidy dollars as explicitly as processing fees. Hiding them in "marketing" is how boards get surprised.
