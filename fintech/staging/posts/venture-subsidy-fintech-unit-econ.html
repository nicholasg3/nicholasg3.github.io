Venture subsidy is not unit economics

Many “efficient” fintech rails only clear while customer acquisition and credit losses are paid by cheap capital. That is a funding regime, not a permanent cost curve.

BASEL / GLOBAL — Fintech pitch culture loves a cost chart. The new rail is cheaper per transfer, faster to fund, and lighter on branches. Sometimes the chart is true. Sometimes the chart omits the line items that made volume possible: paid acquisition, promotional APRs, rewards funded by equity, and credit losses that only look fine while cohorts are young and capital is patient.

Central-bank researchers have been measuring fintech credit growth for years without mistaking growth for free lunch. In the September 2018 BIS Quarterly Review, Stijn Claessens, Jon Frost, Grant Turner, and Feng Zhu documented that fintech credit markets expanded rapidly from a low base, with large cross-country variation and clear policy issues around risk and consumer protection (“Fintech credit markets around the world”). Using Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance and related data, they cited global fintech credit extended on the order of $284 billion in 2016, up from about $11 billion in 2013 in that article’s framing. The point of the series is scale and drivers — not a claim that every platform’s contribution margin was positive.

Volume under venture subsidy is a market share strategy. Unit economics under market cost of capital is a different product.

What the subsidy buys

Venture and growth equity can buy three things that look like product quality in a dashboard. First, customer acquisition: paid install and signup bonuses that push CAC above what organic channels would fund. Second, price: fee waivers, 0% intro periods, and FX “free” marketing that moves spread into less visible lines. Third, credit expansion: looser underwriting or longer terms that raise lifetime value in a model while raising expected losses in a downturn.

None of those tactics is illegal or always irrational. Learning-by-doing and two-sided network effects can justify temporary losses. The analytical error is treating subsidized unit economics as the steady state. When risk-free rates and equity hurdle rates rise, the same cohort tables reverse. Growth slows, CAC stays high, and credit costs surface. Rails that were “efficient” only because someone else paid CAC reveal their true contribution margin.

Fintech credit is not one machine

BIS work also stresses that fintech credit is uneven across countries and that big-tech credit is a related but distinct channel. Later BIS and CCAF-linked research showed volumes large in absolute terms yet still small relative to total private credit stocks in most markets, with China and a few other systems as major swings. That heterogeneity matters for banks and founders. A payments wallet with negative contribution margin in one country is not the same business as a profitable corridor specialist next door. Global “fintech is winning” charts flatten those differences.

Non-bank credit platforms also differ from deposit banks in liability design. Platforms funded by wholesale lines, securitization, or equity do not create bank deposits when they lend in the Bank of England sense. Their constraint is funding markets and investor risk appetite. When that funding is venture-like, credit supply can look elastic. When it is not, the elastic disappears. Calling both “fintech efficiency” confuses a capital-structure choice with a technology cost curve.

A scoreboard that separates subsidy from rail

Metric Useful signal Subsidy disguise
Contribution margin per active user Revenue minus variable product and loss costs Ignores CAC and onboarding bonuses
Payback period on CAC Months to recover acquisition spend Assumes retention that only exists while rewards run
Credit loss by vintage Through-the-cycle loss, not honeymoon months Early vintages under easy underwriting
All-in payment cost Fees + float + FX + support + fraud Headline “0% fee” with spread elsewhere
Funding cost sensitivity P&L at +200–400 bps cost of capital Model frozen at 2021 discount rates

Policy and competition angles

When large platforms price below long-run cost, competitors without cheap capital exit corridors or stop innovating. That can look like consumer surplus in the short run. Supervisors watching fintech credit growth — the BIS authors among them — flag consumer-protection and risk-buildup issues when volume outruns underwriting. Predatory pricing language from competition law is a separate test. The finance test is simpler: can the product clear when equity stops subsidizing CAC and when credit losses mean-revert?

Firms that keep honest internal dashboards run two books. Book A is growth under current capital markets: users, GMV, and brand. Book B is unit economics at a conservative cost of capital with fully loaded CAC, fraud, support, and steady-state credit losses. If Book B never converges, the firm is not discovering a cheaper rail. It is renting market share from limited partners. When LP patience ends, the rail’s true price shows up in fees, tighter credit, or shutdown.

Investors already price this distinction when public fintechs miss path-to-profitability guidance. Private markets relearn it when follow-on rounds demand unit-economics bridges instead of pure growth. Boards that waited for external pressure to build Book B waste years. The earlier the firm separates subsidy from rail cost, the earlier it can choose which products deserve permanent capital and which were only viable as temporary share grabs. Boards that only review GMV and top-line growth are reading the subsidy ledger, not the product ledger.

The industry still produces real efficiency — better APIs, better risk models, better mobile UX. Those gains survive rate cycles. What does not survive is the habit of reading venture-funded growth charts as proof of permanent cost advantage. Fintech credit’s rise in the BIS data is a real market development. It is not a substitute for contribution margins that work when capital is no longer free.

Source notes
