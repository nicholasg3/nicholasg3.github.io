Travel Rule compliance is VASP market structure

FATF Recommendation 16 applied to virtual asset transfers looks like AML paperwork. In production it decides which platforms can send to which counterparties and which vendors intermediate the handshake.

PARIS — Banks have lived with the wire “Travel Rule” for decades: originator and beneficiary information is supposed to travel with a cross-border transfer so intermediaries and law enforcement can see who is paying whom. The Financial Action Task Force extended that logic to virtual assets when it brought virtual asset service providers into the global AML standards. The policy goal is transparency. The market effect is access control.

A VASP that cannot collect, hold, and transmit required data to a counterparty VASP cannot complete a compliant transfer above the relevant threshold. That is not a fine that arrives later. It is a blocked corridor today. Compliance messaging becomes infrastructure. Vendor choice and jurisdiction timing become competitive strategy.

Travel Rule and AML for VASPs reallocate counterparty access and vendor power. They are market structure with a compliance label.

What the FATF standard requires

FATF Recommendation 16 is the payment-transparency rule for wire transfers. In its Updated Guidance for a Risk-Based Approach to Virtual Assets and VASPs (October 2021), the FATF explains how those obligations apply when the transfer is a virtual asset. Originating VASPs must obtain, hold, and transmit required originator and beneficiary information immediately and securely when conducting VA transfers — the industry’s “Travel Rule.” Customer due diligence thresholds for occasional transactions and licensing or registration of VASPs sit alongside that duty under the broader VA/VASP package (Recommendations 15 and 16 and related interpretative material).

The guidance also states a practical problem that became industry infrastructure: there is no exhaustively reliable way, from a blockchain address alone, to identify which VASP controls a beneficiary wallet in all cases. Due diligence on counterparty VASPs, secure transmission channels, and record-keeping obligations therefore matter as much as form fields. Implementation is uneven across countries. Targeted FATF updates have repeatedly noted that many jurisdictions still lag on enforcement and supervisory action focused on Travel Rule compliance — which creates a “sunrise” period where some platforms are ready and others are not.

In June 2025 the FATF agreed further updates to Recommendation 16 on payment transparency, aiming at more consistent data requirements across payment types. Members face a long implementation runway toward the end of the decade for the revised standard. National rules will continue to differ during the transition. Multinational VASPs will run several Travel Rule regimes at once.

Regional rules harden the graph

The European Union did not wait for perfect global coordination. Regulation (EU) 2023/1113 on information accompanying transfers of funds and certain crypto-assets extends transfer-transparency rules to crypto-asset transfers, tightening what crypto-asset service providers must collect and transmit. For EU-facing flows, the regulation is not a soft expectation. It is an operating constraint that forces tooling, process, and sometimes refusal to deal with counterparties that cannot receive or supply data.

Similar hard edges appear wherever licensing regimes make Travel Rule compliance a condition of doing business. The graph of who can pay whom becomes a function of who has implemented interoperable messaging — not only who has a wallet API. Platforms in late-implementing jurisdictions may still serve local users while losing outbound corridors to stricter markets. That is a classic non-tariff barrier pattern applied to crypto market microstructure.

Where market power shows up

Three structural effects follow from mandatory travel of identity data:

Effect Mechanism Who gains leverage
Counterparty rationing Cannot send if counterparty cannot receive Travel Rule data Early-compliant VASPs and their corridors
Vendor concentration Interoperable messaging and discovery services Travel Rule solution providers and networks
Jurisdictional clubs Strict regimes refuse weak counterparties Regulated hubs with mutual recognition
Sunrise friction Partial global implementation Firms that multi-home protocols and legal opinions

None of that requires inventing a scandal. It is the ordinary economics of a network good. Wire-transfer transparency already shaped correspondent banking access; de-risking and KYC friction helped thin corridors. VA Travel Rule imports a cousin of that dynamic into crypto: AML as gatekeeping, with compliance vendors as the new rails. FATF’s own guidance places the burden of building technical solutions on the sector. The sector answered with a market for protocols and SaaS intermediaries. Those intermediaries become choke points for onboarding new VASP pairs.

Self-hosted wallets and peer-to-peer transfers sit at the edge of the standard. The 2021 guidance discusses P2P risk and the limits of applying VASP rules to pure software. Policymakers keep returning to that edge because value that never touches a VASP never carries Travel Rule data. The regulated graph can get cleaner while activity migrates to the unregulated perimeter — another market-structure outcome, not a footnote.

For exchanges and licensed VASPs, the diligence question is no longer only whether the AML policy is thick enough. It is which counterparties the stack can reach in 2026, and which vendor’s outage becomes a corridor outage. Travel Rule compliance remains a legal duty. It is also a map of allowed edges in the VASP network. Markets price that map whether product teams name it or not.

Large exchanges already treat Travel Rule integration as a product and partnership workstream, not a back-office checklist. They multi-home messaging protocols, staff counterparty due diligence, and refuse or delay flows when the other side cannot respond. Smaller VASPs face a harder fixed-cost problem: licensing, tooling, and legal review for each corridor. That cost structure favors scale and favors hubs that already invested. AML policy written as equal rules for all firms still produces unequal market access when implementation capacity differs. That is the market-structure content inside the Travel Rule file.
