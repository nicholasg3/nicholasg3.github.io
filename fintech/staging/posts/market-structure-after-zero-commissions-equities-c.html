Zero commissions redesigned the brokerage stack

Retail apps that charge nothing per equity trade still monetize order flow, balance, and attachment products. That redesign carries conflict and policy risk.

NEW YORK — “Commission-free” became the default label on US retail brokerage apps within a few competitive seasons. Ticket charges that once funded branch networks and call centers fell toward zero for online equity trades. The customer interface got simpler. The revenue stack got more complex. Order flow was sold. Cash was swept. Margin, options, securities lending, and later crypto rails filled the contribution margin that commissions left behind.

The US Securities and Exchange Commission staff put that structure on the public record after the January 2021 meme-stock episode. The Staff Report on Equity and Options Market Structure Conditions in Early 2021 (14 October 2021) is not a rule. It is a staff examination of GameStop-era trading against the market structure that individual investors actually use. Its introduction states a distinction brokers still under-teach: the difference between “free” and “no commissions.”

Zero commission is a price of zero on one line item. It is not a proof that the trade has no cost or that the broker has no conflicting incentive.

What the staff report fixed in view

SEC staff described how individual investors place orders with retail broker-dealers, how those orders are routed to trading centers — exchanges, alternative trading systems, or off-exchange market makers — and how a small set of off-exchange market makers trade profitably with retail order flow. Those market makers negotiate for the right to that flow. Payment for order flow (PFOF) is the cash expression of that bargain. Staff noted that PFOF creates incentives with regard to the end customer whose order flow is sold, and that falling or eliminated commissions may change how often some investors trade, especially when digital engagement practices sit on top of the app.

The report’s market-structure section places those facts next to best-execution duties and to FINRA’s concurrent warnings. FINRA Regulatory Notice 21-23, cited by staff, addresses firms that provide or receive payment for order flow and internalization and the conflicts those arrangements can create. The policy question is not only whether a $0 commission line exists. It is whether routing decisions that fund the free app remain consistent with obtaining best execution for the customer.

The 2019 cascade was a business-model event

Staff recount that many brokers eliminated trading commissions and lowered or eliminated account minimums. Robinhood Financial popularized zero-commission stock trading for a broad retail audience. Competitors followed with their own $0 online equity commission moves. The report’s footnotes point to the press trail: Interactive Brokers’ IBKR Lite, TD Ameritrade, E*Trade, Ally Invest, Charles Schwab, Fidelity, and others announced commission cuts in the late-2010s competitive wave. That cascade was not charity. It was a recognition that retail order flow and adjacent balances were valuable enough to subsidize the ticket price to zero.

Once the ticket is free, growth metrics shift. Accounts, funded accounts, daily average revenue trades, and attachment of options or crypto become the scoreboard. Unit economics move into net interest on cash, securities lending, PFOF, and premium products. Crypto trading inside the same app is a later chapter of the same redesign: free or low-friction UX funded by spreads, order-flow-like wholesaling, or other balance-sheet yields. The equities report does not regulate crypto venues. It does document the retail-broker incentive pattern that crypto-attached apps inherit.

Why meme-stock stress mattered for market structure

GameStop and peer names in January 2021 stressed clearing, capital, and temporary trading restrictions at some retail brokers. Staff analyze short interest, options activity, exchange-traded fund effects, and the role of off-exchange market makers. For this memo the useful extract is simpler. A market structure built on high retail participation, mobile engagement, and zero commissions can move prices and operational risk quickly. Free tickets increase the option value of frequent trading. They do not remove National Securities Clearing Corporation margin calls or the need for broker capital when volatility spikes.

Policy risk is part of the product roadmap

Because PFOF and zero commissions are structural, they attract structural rulemaking risk. Staff reports, enforcement on best execution, and later Commission proposals on market structure all sit in the same file. Brokers that treat $0 commissions as a permanent natural law underprice the chance that order-routing economics change by rule. Brokers that treat PFOF as pure free money underprice the customer-communication cost when “free trading” is re-explained as “your order was sold.”

Product and compliance teams that ship the free ticket in new markets often copy the US app chrome without copying the US disclosure stack. Order-routing reports, PFOF descriptions, and best-execution reviews are not optional chrome in the US. In markets that never had ticket commissions, the same two-sided economics can arrive as “spread only” crypto or CFD apps with even thinner public explanation of who buys the flow. The equities staff report remains a useful primary because it names the conflict in plain language on a regulated stock market, not only on a crypto venue marketing page.

The durable fintech lesson is accounting, not nostalgia for five-dollar tickets. Retail brokerage after zero commissions is a two-sided platform. One side is the individual investor who sees free trades. The other side is the wholesaler, lender, or balance-sheet user who pays for access to flow or balances. SEC staff told the public to mind the difference between free and no commissions. That sentence is still the right opening line for any product review that ships a zero-dollar equity ticket — and for any crypto or tokenized-asset app that copies the same growth motion.
