Merchant acquiring concentration is operational risk

Digital commerce clears through a short list of acquirers, card networks, and cloud hosts. When one path fails, checkout fails in correlated waves. Concentration is a first-class operational risk, not only a fee debate.

ATLANTA — A storefront can look diversified. Multiple SKUs. Multiple countries. Multiple marketing channels. At the payment step, many of those businesses still depend on one acquirer integration, one gateway configuration, and one cloud region behind the processor. The brand owns the customer. The acquirer owns the pipe.

Merchant acquiring is the business of enabling a merchant to accept card and other electronic payments: onboarding, authorization routing, settlement, chargebacks, and risk controls. Scale favors firms that already sit between millions of merchants and the card networks. Rankings publish that concentration every year. Outages and single-path designs make it operational.

Acquirer and network concentration is not only market power. It is correlated downtime at the cash register.

Who sits at the top of the pipe

The Nilson Report’s merchant-acquiring rankings are the industry’s standard league table. In a 13 May 2026 investor release, Fiserv said the Nilson Report named it No. 1 in merchant acquiring in the United States for the 2025 data cycle, first in both total purchase volume and number of transactions. Industry coverage of the same Nilson tables repeatedly places a short list — Fiserv, J.P. Morgan Payments, Worldpay, Global Payments, Elavon, Bank of America — at the top of US acquiring by volume. Exact percentage shares sit behind Nilson’s paywall. The ordinal picture is public enough: a handful of firms process a large share of card purchase volume.

Concentration also stacks in layers. Visa and Mastercard dominate general-purpose network acceptance in many markets. Large processors sit under those networks. Software platforms and gateways sit under the processors. Cloud providers host the software. A merchant that “uses Stripe” or “uses Worldpay” still depends on network rules, issuer decisions, and infrastructure those brands do not fully control. Diversifying marketing agencies does not diversify settlement.

Single path as a design choice

Payment providers themselves describe single-provider dependence as a failure mode. Stripe’s public guidance on payment failover states that relying on one gateway, acquiring bank, or region means any issue in that path can stop payments entirely. The same note treats automatic rerouting to a backup processor or acquiring path as the operational response once failure is confirmed. That is not marketing against competitors. It is an admission that high uptime on one stack is not the same as resilience across stacks.

Merchants learn the difference during incidents. Authorization rates fall. Carts abandon. Support queues fill with “payment failed” tickets that are not fraud and not inventory. Finance teams scramble to reconcile partial captures. The economic loss is not only the processor’s SLA credit. It is the gross margin that never cleared while the pipe was dark.

What concentration changes in risk reviews

Classic merchant risk work focuses on chargebacks, MCC mix, and rolling reserves. Concentration risk asks different questions. How many acquirers can the treasury team actually settle through in seventy-two hours? Is the gateway abstraction real, or is mid-transaction failover untested? Which cloud region hosts the primary processor’s API endpoints that the storefront calls? What share of e-commerce volume sits behind one network token vault?

Layer Concentrated actors (illustrative) Failure symptom
Card networks Visa, Mastercard (plus regional schemes) Wide authorization or settlement disruption
Merchant acquirers / processors Fiserv, JPM Payments, Worldpay, Global Payments, others Merchant-specific or portfolio outages
Gateways / platforms Large PSPs and commerce platforms Checkout API errors across many brands
Cloud infrastructure Major public clouds hosting payment stacks Correlated multi-merchant downtime

Regulators and overseers of financial market infrastructures have long treated concentration and operational resilience as first-class themes in payment system oversight — the CPMI-IOSCO Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures frame operational reliability and interdependent risks for systemically important infrastructures. Merchant acquiring is not always supervised as an FMI, but the merchant’s cash register still inherits the topology. A digital retailer with thin margins can tolerate fee competition. It cannot tolerate a single untested path on Black Friday.

M&A keeps pushing the same direction. Large processors buy other large processors to gain volume, technology, and merchant portfolios. Each deal can improve product breadth. It can also shorten the list of independent settlement paths. Boards that only track interchange and take rate miss the resilience ledger: how many independent ways money can still clear when one logo is offline.

Acquiring concentration will not unwind because rankings are published. Scale is efficient. The operational response is deliberate multi-homing where volume justifies it, tested failover, and risk reports that treat processor outage scenarios as seriously as chargeback spikes. The customer never sees the acquirer name. The merchant still lives or dies by whether that name can authorize.

Enterprise merchants already negotiate multi-acquirer setups for geographic coverage and rate shopping. Mid-market and long-tail commerce often cannot. They inherit platform defaults: one PSP, one dashboard, one path to Visa and Mastercard. Platform concentration then multiplies acquirer concentration. When a major commerce stack and a major processor share the same cloud dependency, a regional infrastructure event can look like a payments crisis even if card networks themselves are healthy. Resilience reviews that stop at “our processor’s status page is green” miss that stack. The practical test is simple. On a peak sales day, can a second path clear enough volume to keep the business alive if the first path fails for four hours?

Fee benchmarking still matters. Interchange, scheme fees, and acquirer markup decide unit economics. They do not answer continuity. Boards that only review basis points will miss a more expensive failure mode: zero authorization rate across an entire channel. Concentration risk belongs next to cyber and liquidity on the operational risk register for any firm whose revenue clears through cards.
