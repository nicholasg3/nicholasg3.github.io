Network School as a Silicon Valley bubble in ASEAN

Importing SV norms into ASEAN sites can create value for mobile founders and friction for host politics. Talent density and enclave are two readings of the same room.

Seed tweet

JOHOR / SINGAPORE STRAIT — Silicon Valley is not only a place. It is a portable bundle of norms: English-first founder talk, equity culture, techno-optimist status games, and a belief that the right room of people can bootstrap institutions. Network School packaged that bundle as a paid community. Its public site, ns.com, calls the project a frontier community for techno-optimists and a “startup society,” with membership from about $1,500 a month including lodging, meals, and gym access. The product is society-as-a-service for people who already live partly online.

For a subset of founders and remote workers, that offer is rational. Cross-border collaboration is expensive when it depends on flights and weak weak-ties. A dense temporary campus can compress networking costs. Speakers and peer density are the product. Institutional distance from the host society is not always a bug in that design. It can be the feature that keeps the room high-context for people who share the same meme set.

What looks like talent density from inside a co-living floor can look like an enclave from outside the gate.

The norms that travel

Network School’s self-description is explicit about the culture it imports. Members are framed as remote workers, digital nomads, creators, and engineers. The intellectual backdrop includes Balaji Srinivasan’s network-state writing — the idea that online communities can exit and rebuild governance rather than only reform existing states. Whether that philosophy is taken literally or as marketing poetry, the operational culture is recognizable to anyone who has spent time in Bay Area founder spaces: demo-day energy, English as the default, health optimization aesthetics, and a high tolerance for experimental social technology.

Those norms create value when the customer is the mobile professional class. They create friction when the host jurisdiction expects a different story: local employment, ordinary commercial licensing, immigration transparency, and political quiet. Institutional-distance research in international business has a bland name for the same gap. Organizational practices that fit one home environment travel poorly when the host’s rules, legitimacy myths, and enforcement tools differ.

The price point itself is a selection device. At roughly $1,500 a month all-in, the community filters for people who can pay for proximity to a specific subculture. That is normal for private education and co-living products. It is abnormal as a proxy for “ASEAN founder demand.” Most Southeast Asian operators and engineers do not budget that way. Measuring the region through a paid bubble therefore samples the intersection of remote-work income and network-state aesthetics, not the median startup in Jakarta or Ho Chi Minh City.

Why Forest City was a revealing host

Network School did not land in a neutral warehouse district. It operated in Forest City, the Country Garden mega-project in Johor already associated with foreign-buyer politics and thin occupancy. BBC reporting in 2023 documented low occupancy and the 2018 Mahathir-era objection to a city framed as built for foreigners. A second imported community — this time Silicon Valley rather than Chinese second-home demand — arrived on a site already coded as extra-territorial in local debate.

That context matters for interpretation. A founder bubble in downtown Kuala Lumpur or Singapore’s central business district still sits inside a thick host economy. A founder bubble inside a half-empty master plan near the border sits inside a thinner legitimacy shell. The same membership fee buys different political optics.

When the host state reasserted control

In July 2026, Malaysian authorities made the friction public. The Iskandar Puteri City Council revoked Network School’s business licence and ordered activities to stop, with Johor’s chief minister supporting the move in statements covered by The Business Times and TechNode Global. Reporting described immigration investigations after social-media claims about participants, and licensing findings about premises use. Some local reports said immigration officials found no passport-misuse evidence even as the business licence still fell. Process details will keep being litigated in public. The strategic lesson does not require a conspiracy theory: host governments can shut a community product through ordinary municipal and immigration tools.

What the bubble is good for — and what it is not

A Silicon Valley cultural bubble in ASEAN can still be a useful product for a narrow customer. It can accelerate deal flow among people who already share US tech norms. It can serve as a temporary staging ground near Singapore without Singapore rents. It can test whether a network-state aesthetic finds paying members. Those are real functions.

It is a weak instrument for national innovation policy. Host states that want technology transfer usually want local firms, local hires, tax residency, and political quiet. A campus that optimizes for global nomads optimizes against some of those goals. When organizers treat local licensing as a temporary inconvenience rather than a core customer, the host eventually becomes the unsatisfied stakeholder with the harder power.

The measurement error is common in founder media. Photos of whiteboards and gym classes look like “ASEAN tech rising.” Licence revocation and immigration probes look like “ASEAN tech blocked.” Both readings miss the institutional point. The bubble was never a random sample of Malaysian or ASEAN demand. It was a priced room for people who already live in a transpacific culture. That room can be valuable. It is not a national market. And when it sits on a shoreline already loaded with foreigner politics, the host state’s reading of the same density is the binding constraint.
