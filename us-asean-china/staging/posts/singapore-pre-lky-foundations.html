Singapore’s foundations predate the LKY myth

Founder stories compress path dependence. The island’s free-port design, legal rails, and entrepôt role were already old when independence arrived in 1965.

Seed tweet

SINGAPORE — Public memory often starts the national story in the mid-1960s. Lee Kuan Yew and the People’s Action Party built a developmental state under real pressure: separation, conscription, housing, industrial policy, and a security environment that left little margin for error. That record is substantial. It is also incomplete as an origin story for the place as a commercial node.

The island’s modern trading-post chapter opened on 6 February 1819, when Stamford Raffles and Malay rulers formalized a British East India Company presence. Singapore’s National Heritage Board Roots story on the founding of modern Singapore places that treaty in a longer geography: a natural harbour on the route between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea, contested by Dutch and British commercial systems. The settlement was not invented as a closed colonial plantation. It was built as a trade station in a rivalrous archipelago.

Leadership after 1965 reorganized the state. It did not invent the free-port geography underneath it.

Free port before republic

Raffles and early administrators treated Singapore as a free port. That choice was deliberate. Port duties that loaded competing harbours were the foil. The National Library Board’s article on Stamford Raffles’s career and contributions notes policies and harbour regulations that helped establish the settlement as a free port. Heritage accounts describe rapid population growth as regional merchants tested the new node. Roots records that by 1821 the island’s population had risen from roughly one thousand to about five thousand — a crude early signal that the free-port offer was already moving people and goods.

The free-port rule mattered because it selected for a certain kind of city. Open harbour rules pulled Bugis, Chinese, Arab, Indian, and European traders into a shared commercial surface. English commercial practice and British legal administration layered on top. None of that is a claim that colonial rule was benign. It is a claim about path dependence. Once the island’s returns came from openness and intermediating other people’s trade, later governments inherited a comparative advantage that was hard to replace with a closed-economy design.

Institutions stacked before independence

In 1826 Singapore entered the Straits Settlements with Penang and Malacca, again per the Roots founding narrative. That bundling put the island inside a British administrative and legal package. Residents such as William Farquhar and John Crawfurd ran early municipal and commercial order; Roots notes Crawfurd’s role in securing investment and helping set up a legal system. The precise statutes changed across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The institutional family — common-law courts, English as a commercial language, a port-city civil service culture — did not start in 1965.

By the time Singapore separated from Malaysia on 9 August 1965, it already sat on a deep stack: harbour works, a multi-ethnic labour force oriented to trade services, British-derived legal practice, and a habit of living off external demand. Independence rewrote sovereignty and domestic politics. It did not erase those rails. Industrial policy later moved the economy up the value chain into manufacturing, finance, and logistics services. Those moves used the old node; they did not replace geography with rhetoric.

The same path-dependence lens helps separate two kinds of “Singapore exceptionalism” that get mixed in pitch decks. One is the claim that wise men willed a modern city into being on empty ground. The other is the claim that a small open economy on a choke-point strait, running open commercial rules and credible courts, compounds advantages over long horizons. The second claim survives contact with the 1819–1826 record. The first claim needs the later state — and still needs the earlier harbour.

Layer What was already present What post-1965 leadership still had to do
Geography Harbour position on major Asian sea routes Defend access, invest in capacity, keep reliability high
Trade rule Free-port commercial design from the early settlement Update customs, industrial estates, and services regulation
Law and administration British colonial legal-administrative practice Nationalize, professionalize, and adapt for a sovereign state
Demography of trade Multi-ethnic merchant and labour communities Housing, language policy, national service, social compact
External demand Entrepôt role dependent on regional and global trade Diversify into manufacturing, finance, and high-skill services

Why the great-man compression misleads operators

Founder myths are useful for nation-building and for leadership training. They are poor market-entry guides. Operators who treat Singapore as a pure “policy genius” story underweight what was already locked in: openness as default, English as a business interface, courts that foreign capital can model, and a population long practiced at serving other people’s supply chains. Operators who treat Singapore as pure colonial residue underweight what the independent state actually did — land policy, education, industrial targeting, anti-corruption capacity, and security.

Historical institutionalism has a plainer phrase for the same pattern. Early choices raise the returns to continuing along a path. Singapore’s path was entrepôt openness under imperial competition, then sovereign developmental policy on top of that base. Paul Pierson’s path-dependence frame is a theory hook, not a Singapore archive. It still helps separate two questions founders often fuse. What did the post-independence state choose? What did geography and inherited institutions already price in?

The practical read for regional strategy is modest. Singapore’s reliability is not only a personality cult. It is a long accumulation of harbour economics, legal continuity, and state capacity that used those assets rather than fighting them. Lee Kuan Yew’s generation mattered for the second half of that sentence. The first half was already on the map in 1819.

Compressing both halves into one man produces bad forecasts. It overstates how easily another city can copy “the Singapore model” by copying a speech. It understates how much of the model is path-dependent infrastructure that took more than a century to stack. For anyone building across ASEAN, the useful lesson is institutional: know which advantages are recent policy and which are deep rails that predate the independence myth.
