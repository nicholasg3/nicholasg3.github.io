K-shaped America and elite mobility narratives

Elite cosmopolitan mobility stories can mask domestic distribution fights. Founder optionality and median household constraints are different ledgers.

Seed tweet

WASHINGTON / NEW YORK — American public conversation often runs on a single national mood. Markets are “strong,” consumers are “resilient,” or the country is “struggling.” Distributional data keep refusing the singular. Since the pandemic, analysts have reached for a letter: the K-shaped economy, where some households and sectors rise while others lag. The metaphor is crude. The underlying split is measurable enough to discipline founder narratives that treat US optionality as a universal national experience.

On 1 May 2026, Federal Reserve Bank of New York researchers published a Liberty Street Economics post on who drives retail spending. Using Numerator consumer data benchmarked against Census retail series, they found that since 2023 retail spending growth has been driven by high-income households — those earning more than $125,000 a year. In real terms, only the high-income group showed consistent spending growth over the period they emphasize. Low-income real spending declined for part of the window and only later regained earlier levels; middle-income real spending stalled for long stretches. Aggregate “resilient consumer” headlines can be true while the composition of that resilience is narrow.

Founder mobility and median budgets can both be real — they are not the same American story.

Wealth skew under the spending split

Spending is a flow. Wealth is a stock. The Federal Reserve’s Survey of Consumer Finances remains the standard household balance-sheet instrument. The Board’s report on the 2019–2022 SCF found that real median net worth rose sharply — about 37 percent to roughly $192,900 in 2022 dollars — while mean net worth remained far higher than the median. That gap is the statistical signature of concentration: averages travel with the upper tail. Families at the top of the distribution hold financial assets and housing equity that both buffer inflation and fund mobility. Families near the median can see wealth gains and still face tight monthly budgets when prices for food, rent, and credit move against them.

The New York Fed authors note that the K-shape they measure in spending is period-specific. Earlier windows, including parts of the immediate post-COVID recovery, did not show the same high-income dominance in retail growth. Pandemic transfers and a tight labour market at the bottom of the wage distribution once supported broader spending. When those supports faded, composition changed. The letter K is not a permanent property of America. It is a description of a recent divergence that matters for anyone reading national aggregates as if they were uniform.

Elite mobility as a second narrative

Alongside the distributional data runs a different story popular in founder and professional circles: high mobility, multi-city lives, equity upside, and passport optionality. That story is not invented. US high-income households do have more capacity to reallocate spending, absorb asset volatility, and buy geographic flexibility. What the story cannot do is stand in for the median household. When media and conference stages treat founder optionality as the national condition, they perform a harmony narrative — a claim that the country’s direction is shared — while the spending and wealth ledgers show a fork.

The seed’s “lusotropicalism” label is useful only as a warning about harmony talk. Elite cosmopolitan narratives can sound inclusive and frictionless while the measured economy is bifurcated. Portuguese colonial history is not the load-bearing claim here. Two scoreboards are.

Why the split matters for regional firms

Firms that sell into the United States from Singapore, or that hire US remote talent into ASEAN ventures, often live socially inside the upper arm of the K. Their customers, employees, and LPs may also sit there. Product decisions that assume universal consumer resilience will overfit to high-income baskets. Hiring narratives that assume every US worker has founder-class mobility will misprice benefits, location, and retention. Political risk models that treat “America” as one demand bloc will miss how distribution fights show up in regulation, tariffs, and industrial policy rhetoric.

When a deck says the US consumer is strong, the useful question is which income band’s real spending series is doing the work. When a founder story celebrates mobility, the useful question is whether the product depends on median households or on the same slice that already funds luxury travel and equity-financed optionality. When wealth medians rise in SCF headlines, the mean-median gap still has to be checked before anyone declares broad balance-sheet health.

Associated Press explainers have noted how quickly the K-shape phrase moved from social media into executive and Fed-adjacent speech. Popularity is not proof. The New York Fed’s contribution is to put composition under the phrase with a defined income cut and a retail series. That is enough to separate two true statements that often get fused. High-income America can fund mobility and consumption growth. Median America can face a different monthly math. Strategy that confuses the two is a measurement error with a good wardrobe.
