MAS Granted Coinbase's Wholesale Asks and Held the Line on Retail

Hassan Ahmed's April 2025 Coinbase blog post asked Singapore for seven policy moves. Fifteen months later, the wholesale asks shipped and the retail asks did not — a split too consistent to read as a stall.

SINGAPORE — In April 2025, Hassan Ahmed, Coinbase's country director for Singapore, published a Coinbase blog post arguing that the city-state was at risk of falling behind Hong Kong, the UAE, and the UK on crypto policy. The post, "What Singapore can do to be a global crypto leader," named seven specific moves: a national digital asset strategy, a strategic Bitcoin position, eased retail access to crypto trading, relaxed marketing rules for licensed exchanges, more research and development spending, expanded Skillsfuture pathways into crypto and digital-asset roles, and a crypto-specific regulatory sandbox. Fifteen months on, with Ahmed set to appear on the NUS FinTech Lab podcast on 6 August, the record is specific enough to score.

Four of the seven asks moved, and they cluster tightly: wholesale settlement infrastructure, institutional tokenization, and bank-facing capital rules. The Monetary Authority of Singapore launched the BLOOM initiative on 16 October 2025 to extend settlement into tokenized bank liabilities and regulated stablecoins, building on the earlier Project Orchid work on a digital Singapore dollar. It expanded Project Guardian and the related Global Layer One programme on 1 April 2026, closing GL1's first phase — 24 participating financial institutions — and adding the Global Financial Markets Association, the International Capital Market Association, and the International Swaps and Derivatives Association as its first global industry-association members. MAS Managing Director Chia Der Jiun told the Singapore FinTech Festival in November 2025 that stablecoin legislation was being drafted for a mid-2026 target. And on 17 April 2026, MAS opened a consultation on the prudential treatment of cryptoassets issued on permissionless blockchains, proposing that such assets are not automatically barred from lighter capital treatment if risk controls are in place — a technical concession to banks that want to hold tokenized assets without a capital penalty.

Every ask that moved kept the transaction inside a regulated bank or an MAS-vetted institution. Every ask that didn't would have put a token directly in a retail customer's hand.

What stayed exactly where it was

The other three asks are unmoved, and the pattern among them is the same in reverse. Singapore's 2022–23 retail restrictions are still in force: no public advertising of crypto services on transport, billboards, or mass media; no local credit-card funding for token purchases; no retail lending or staking, which MAS has repeatedly called "generally not suitable" for retail investors; and a mandatory risk-awareness assessment before a retail customer can even open a trading account. Ahmed's marketing ask — let licensed firms advertise so consumers can tell them apart from offshore platforms — has not been granted either. There is no sign of a Singapore sovereign or MAS-linked strategic Bitcoin reserve; GIC's only visible crypto-adjacent position is an investment in the blockchain-analytics firm Chainalysis, not a Bitcoin treasury. And while MAS has run a general fintech regulatory sandbox since 2016, no crypto-specific sandbox — the version Ahmed's post asked for — has been stood up.

Ahmed's own account of the region, published as a CoinDesk "Crypto for Advisors" piece on 28 May 2026, implicitly concedes the split. The piece argues Asia is not catching up on crypto but already integrated, citing $12.5 trillion in regional stablecoin transaction volume in 2025 and walking through Singapore's Project Ubin (2016), the Payment Services Act, Project Guardian (2022), and BLOOM (2025) as a coherent decade-long build. It does not repeat the retail-access argument from a year earlier. The institutional story had become the story worth telling.

The competitors moved the other way

The two jurisdictions Ahmed named as pacesetters have both, since April 2025, put more weight on the retail-facing side of the ledger than Singapore has. Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Commission published its five-pillar ASPIRe roadmap — Access, Safeguards, Products, Infrastructure, Relationships — on 19 February 2025, aimed at institutional access and product breadth. On 10 April 2026, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority granted the territory's first two stablecoin issuer licences: one to HSBC, one to Anchorpoint, a joint venture of Standard Chartered Bank, HKT, and Animoca Brands. Both licensees plan to launch Hong Kong dollar-referenced stablecoins in the second half of 2026 — a consumer- and merchant-facing product, not a wholesale settlement rail. The UAE, meanwhile, has consolidated a multi-regulator structure across the Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority for Dubai activity, the Central Bank of the UAE as sole regulator for payment tokens, and the Securities and Commodities Authority for investment-type tokens federally, with VARA and the SCA now sharing a unified register so a Dubai licence carries federal visibility. That is friction reduction aimed squarely at exchanges and the retail customers who use them.

Reading the split through Kane, not through capture

Edward Kane's "regulatory dialectic," coined in the 1980s, describes regulation as a two-way adaptive process: regulated firms innovate around a constraint, and regulators respond to whatever new risk that innovation creates, in a repeating cycle rather than a one-time ruling. MAS's four-for-three split reads as exactly that kind of selective, adaptive response rather than either full capture or blanket refusal. Where Ahmed's asks moved the risk further inside regulated banks and MAS-vetted institutions — a tokenized liability sitting on a licensed bank's balance sheet, a fixed-income settlement workstream run with ISDA — MAS said yes. Where the ask would have put a token directly into an unsophisticated retail customer's hands, it said no, and kept saying no through three full budget cycles.

That pattern argues against reading MAS as captured by the industry lobbying it, in the sense George Stigler's 1971 model of regulation-as-a-good-supplied-to-demanders, later refined by Sam Peltzman's 1976 extension in the Journal of Law and Economics, would predict. Capture theory expects the regulator to grant what the best-organized, most vocal regulated interest wants most. Coinbase's most publicly argued ask — ease retail access, because current rules "blur the line between regulated and unlicensed players" — is precisely the one that did not move. What moved instead served a different constituency: banks, clearing houses, and MAS's own settlement infrastructure. Dirk Zetzsche, Ross Buckley, Douglas Arner, and Janos Barberis's 2017 Fordham Journal of Corporate and Financial Law paper on regulatory sandboxes is useful here too, precisely because MAS did not build the crypto-specific sandbox instrument the paper describes and Ahmed asked for — sandboxes are built to let a regulator watch a new activity up close before deciding on it, and MAS has chosen to keep crypto retail activity at arm's length rather than inside a supervised box.

Second place as a choice, not a failure

None of this means Singapore has lost the race Ahmed described in April 2025 — it means Singapore may not be running that race. Hong Kong and the UAE are competing for retail-facing volume: exchange listings, consumer stablecoins, a Dubai-registered platform serving individual traders. Singapore is competing for something narrower and, on the wholesale side, arguably harder to win: the settlement layer that banks, clearing houses, and fund managers route real balance-sheet risk through. BLOOM's roster, GL1's new institutional members, and a bank-capital consultation are bets on being the jurisdiction institutions trust with the plumbing, not the jurisdiction with the highest retail account-opening rate. Ahmed's own May 2026 framing — Singapore's Ubin-to-BLOOM sequence as evidence of coherent decade-long design, not catch-up — reads, whether he intended it this way or not, as an acknowledgment that MAS was never going to grant the retail ask on his timeline. A regulator that has said no to the same request three years running, while saying yes to every request that keeps the risk inside institutions it already supervises, is not stalling. It has already answered the question Coinbase's scorecard poses. The answer is a ceiling on retail, not a delay on strategy.
