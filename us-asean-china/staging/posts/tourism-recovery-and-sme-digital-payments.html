Tourism recovery reloads SME payment seasonality

As visitors return to Southeast Asian destinations, small merchants meet a payments problem that is seasonal, cross-border, and increasingly QR-native. National rails — not only private wallets — now sit in the middle of the trip.

BANGKOK — Tourism is a payments industry wearing a leisure costume. Hotels, drivers, restaurants, dive shops, and market stalls live on seasonal cashflow. When international arrivals collapse, as they did in 2020–2021, acquiring volumes and working-capital needs collapse with them. When arrivals recover, the same firms face the opposite stress: peak-season settlement, foreign cards, and now foreign QR wallets scanning local codes.

UN Tourism (formerly UNWTO) has tracked the multi-year climb back from the pandemic trough. In a January 2025 release, UN Tourism reported an estimated 1.4 billion international tourist arrivals in 2024 — about 99 percent of pre-pandemic levels globally — while Asia and the Pacific reached about 316 million arrivals, still only 87 percent of pre-pandemic levels despite rapid recovery from 2023. The regional pattern remains uneven by country and by source market. Chinese outbound, European long-haul, and intra-ASEAN travel do not move on one calendar. For SME payments, the useful fact is simpler. Visitor clocks reassert seasonality in merchant cashflow. Digital rails change how that cash clears. They do not abolish the season.

Tourism-linked SME payments are seasonal credit and acquiring problems. QR interoperability is infrastructure for that season, not a replacement for it.

National QR rails became tourist infrastructure

Thailand’s PromptPay system and Indonesia’s QRIS standard are central-bank-led retail payment projects that outgrew domestic P2P use. The Bank of Thailand’s cross-border payment pages describe linkages that let tourists and merchants make and accept instant cross-border QR payments. Thai users can pay abroad with mobile apps; foreign users can pay Thai merchants under the linked schemes. Indonesia is an explicit partner corridor: BOT explains that Thai apps can scan QRIS codes in Indonesia and Indonesian apps can scan Thai QR codes for goods and services.

Bank Indonesia’s QRIS documentation presents the Quick Response Code Indonesian Standard as the national QR payment code for merchant transactions, with cross-border arrangements involving partner countries including Thailand, Malaysia, and Singapore. Secondary industry summaries of QRIS scale — tens of millions of users and merchants by 2024 — should be treated as order-of-magnitude signals from banking-association and press tallies unless a dated BI statistical release is attached. The institutional fact does not require a precise merchant census. A national standard QR exists, SMEs display it, and tourists are a designed use case for cross-border extensions.

That design matters. A decade ago, the tourist payment problem was mostly cards, cash, and a few closed-loop wallets. Now a Thai visitor in Bali or an Indonesian visitor in Chiang Mai can, in linked corridors, pay with a home-app QR workflow. The merchant’s acquiring problem becomes which codes to display, which acquirer settles when, and how refunds and disputes work when the payer’s bank sits across a border.

Seasonality is the credit story

Stress What peaks with visitors What breaks if ignored
Acquiring mix Cards, domestic QR, cross-border QR Missing a rail means lost basket at the stall
Settlement timing Daily cash need for inventory and wages T+N settlement during peak week creates liquidity holes
Chargebacks / disputes Foreign cards and travel claims Thin SMEs cannot fund long dispute cycles
Off-season trough Low volume, fixed costs continue Credit products built only on peak GMV overstate capacity
FX and scheme fees Foreign spend share rises with tourism Headline “cashless” growth hides net margin

Fintech underwriting that uses peak-month digital payment volume as a straight-line annual revenue proxy will mis-price tourism SMEs. The same merchant that clears aggressively in December may be thin in May. Cross-border QR increases the share of electronic receipts in peak months. It also increases operational complexity: more rails, more reconciliation files, more failure modes when a tourist’s app and a stall’s printed code disagree.

Recovery restores the peak

Tourism recovery restores the peak. It does not automatically professionalize SME finance. Many merchants still stack cash, domestic bank transfer, one or two wallets, and a card terminal if the rent justifies it. Central-bank QR standards lower the cost of displaying a single interoperable code. They leave intact the need for working capital between seasons, and they leave scheme fees on foreign spend in place.

Payment companies and banks that frame “tourism digital payments” as a pure adoption story over-index on wallet downloads and under-index on settlement, dispute, and credit design. The grounded product problems are seasonal underwriting, multi-rail acquiring, and cross-border exception handling. Those problems intensify as visitor numbers recover and as ASEAN payment connectivity initiatives add more bilateral QR links.

Asia-Pacific’s lag versus global full recovery is itself a payments signal. Destinations still climbing toward 2019 arrival levels will see QR and card mix shift in bursts when a source market reopens or a visa rule changes. Acquirers that model a smooth S-curve will mis-staff support and mis-fund merchant advances. The recovery is real. It is also lumpy — which is exactly when seasonal credit products either protect SMEs or strand them.

Seasonal credit on national rails

Tourism-linked SME payments in Southeast Asia are seasonal credit and acquiring problems shaped by national QR infrastructure. PromptPay and QRIS, plus their cross-border linkages as described by BOT and BI, are primary institutional facts. UN Tourism’s 2024 recovery figures supply the demand clock: global arrivals near full recovery, Asia-Pacific still catching up. Merchants who live on visitors need rails that work at the stall and credit that survives the trough. Digital payments change the form of the receipt. They do not flatten the calendar.
