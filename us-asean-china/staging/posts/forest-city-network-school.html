Forest City and Network School: capital meets host politics

Mega-projects fail politically when tenant cultures and host-state legitimacy diverge. Johor’s Forest City is a case of capital, immigration politics, and imported communities colliding.

Seed tweet

JOHOR — Forest City was sold as a future metropolis on reclaimed land at the southern tip of Malaysia. China’s Country Garden unveiled the project in the mid-2010s as a large eco-city concept, often framed in Belt and Road coverage. The pitch included homes, offices, leisure, and a long-horizon population target measured in the high hundreds of thousands to about one million people, depending on the sales version. The site sat close enough to Singapore to trade on border adjacency. It sat far enough from daily Malaysian urban life to need its own demand story.

By December 2023, that story had already cracked in public. BBC News reported that only about 15 percent of the full project had been built and that occupancy estimates for the total planned development sat just over 1 percent. The same piece described empty corridors, closed mall units, and renters who treated the place as a ghost town. Country Garden told the BBC it remained optimistic. The physical ledger on the ground told a thinner story.

Master-planned cities sell units. Host states sell legitimacy — and they can withdraw it.

A city built for a buyer who could not fully arrive

Forest City’s commercial design leaned on Chinese middle-class buyers seeking property abroad, not only on local Malaysian end-users. BBC reporting and contemporaneous regional coverage framed prices and marketing around that foreign demand. Local political reaction followed the same line. In 2018, then-prime minister Mahathir Mohamad restricted visas for Chinese buyers and objected to a city he characterized as built for foreigners, as the BBC account records.

That was a state–capital bargain under stress. Developers needed cross-border buyers and capital. Malaysian politics needed a project that did not look like demographic outsourcing of a coastal enclave. Visa policy became a product feature. Currency controls in China, pandemic travel freezes, and Country Garden’s wider balance-sheet crisis later compounded the demand shock. The Business Times and other regional outlets traced the same stack of hurdles: environmental pauses early on, Singapore concerns about the border project, Chinese outbound property limits, and thin occupancy.

A mega-project is a political object as soon as its tenants are coded as foreign. Sales brochures cannot substitute for host legitimacy. Cash-flow models that assume continuous foreign pre-sales also break when the foreign state tightens outbound capital or when a domestic election reframes the shoreline as a sovereignty issue.

Network School as a second tenant culture

Into that partially occupied landscape came a different import. Network School, associated with investor Balaji Srinivasan and the “network state” intellectual frame, opened a co-living and co-learning community in Forest City in 2024. The project’s own site, ns.com, describes a frontier community for techno-optimists and prices membership from about $1,500 a month with roommates, including meals, gym access, and accommodations. The product is not a Malaysian township scheme. It is a portable founder subculture sold as society-as-a-service.

For some participants, that package is exactly the point: density of remote workers, English-language founder norms, and a temporary city of people who already live online. For host politics, the same package can read as an enclave. Forest City had already been politicized as a foreigner-facing property project. Network School layered a second imported culture on the same ground: Silicon Valley and crypto-adjacent social technology rather than Chinese second-home demand.

In July 2026, the collision became administrative. Johor’s Iskandar Puteri City Council revoked Network School’s business licence and ordered activities to cease, with Johor Chief Minister Onn Hafiz Ghazi supporting the decision in public statements carried by The Business Times, TechNode Global, and other outlets. Reporting described an immigration probe after social-media claims about participants, and licensing findings that premises use did not match office-use conditions. Local coverage also noted immigration authorities saying they found no passport-misuse evidence even as the licence still fell. The precise legal theory can be debated. The political fact is clearer: the host government retained the kill switch.

What the host kill switch prices

Special economic zones and master plans often advertise regulatory exception as the product. Forest City shows the reverse risk. When the exception depends on foreign buyers and foreign lifestyles, the host state’s immigration and licensing tools become product-risk factors equal to interest rates or steel costs. Country Garden could pour concrete. It could not pour visas. Network School could densify talent for a season. It could not outvote a city council.

The scoreboard for ASEAN site scouting is concrete. Who is the real tenant? What language and politics do they import? Which local institution can shut the doors without a court battle measured in years? Forest City answered those questions twice — once as empty towers, once as a revoked community licence.

Mega-projects and imported subcultures can coexist with host development goals when the bargain is explicit and the tenants are politically legible. When capital, culture, and sovereignty diverge, the host state usually reasserts the last word. Johor’s shoreline made that sequence unusually easy to photograph.
