US–China tech controls as market-entry parameters for SEA founders

Export controls and model-compute limits are product and partnership constraints for firms operating between Washington and Beijing.

Seed tweet

SINGAPORE — Southeast Asian technology firms like to describe their geography as optionality. Singapore capital, Indonesian users, Vietnamese engineers, Chinese supply chains, American cloud and models. That portfolio still works for many products. It works less well when the product touches advanced computing, semiconductor tools, high-bandwidth memory, or customers that sit on a US Entity List. Then the portfolio becomes a compliance design problem with geopolitical owners.

On 7 October 2022, the US Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security issued interim final rules restricting the People’s Republic of China’s ability to obtain and manufacture certain advanced semiconductors and related manufacturing items. Congressional Research Service product R48642 summarizes the arc: new Export Control Classification Numbers for advanced logic and computing items, Entity List updates, and expanded foreign direct product rules that pull foreign-made items with US technology inputs into US jurisdiction. The stated aim was national security — limiting advanced AI and military-relevant computing — not a general trade war with every Southeast Asian laptop vendor.

For dual-use founders in ASEAN, export controls are a product requirement masquerading as foreign policy.

The control stack kept rising

BIS did not treat October 2022 as a one-time announcement. Updates in October 2023 tightened thresholds and closed gaps around chips engineered to sit just under prior limits — the public discussion of Nvidia’s China-market A800 and H800 designs is the textbook example in CRS and trade press. Further packages in 2024 continued the “small yard, high fence” approach. On 2 December 2024, BIS announced another package with new controls on semiconductor manufacturing equipment and software tools, high-bandwidth memory, Entity List additions measured in the hundreds of party names across the package’s companion actions, and foreign direct product rules aimed at advanced-node production.

Official language is consistent. Secretary Gina Raimondo and national security officials framed the measures as impairing PRC indigenization of advanced technologies with military applications, while claiming to leave ordinary commercial trade outside the fence. Whether the fence is “small” is a political argument. For firms near the yard, the operational fact is simpler: licence requirements, presumption-of-denial patterns, diversion red flags, and Entity List screening are now permanent product constraints.

Why Southeast Asia sits in the middle

ASEAN hubs are not the named primary target of the chip rules. They are where multi-jurisdiction firms live. A Singapore holding company, a Malaysia or Vietnam assembly relationship, a Chinese model-training partner, and US cloud GPUs can appear in one architecture diagram. Under the Export Administration Regulations, US-origin items, certain foreign direct products, and US-person support for advanced PRC fabs can all trigger obligations. Re-export and diversion risk is not theoretical. It is why BIS publishes red-flag guidance and why banks and cloud providers ask end-use questions that sound like customs forms.

Malaysia, Vietnam, and Singapore all sit in semiconductor and electronics value chains that touch US tools, Taiwanese or Korean process know-how, and PRC demand. That makes the region a natural place for both legitimate multi-country manufacturing and for diversion concern. Founders do not need a smuggling plot to be affected. They need a customer in Shenzhen, a GPU allocation in US cloud, and a contract manufacturer whose beneficial owners change. The paperwork follows the topology of the supply graph, not the founder’s passport.

Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman’s “weaponized interdependence” frame captures the strategic logic without requiring agreement on US China policy. States that sit on chokepoints in network infrastructures — finance, data, advanced chips — can convert interdependence into coercion. BIS export controls are one such conversion. SEA founders do not choose whether the chokepoint exists. They choose whether their product roadmap assumes unrestricted access to the highest US compute stack and the deepest PRC manufacturing stack at the same time.

Market entry as licence design

Some SEA go-to-market plans treat export controls as a US–China story that local firms can watch from the sidelines. Stronger plans treat controls as market-entry parameters equal to data localization or payments licensing. Which countries and customers are in scope for advanced compute products? Which partnerships with PRC tool or model firms create knowledge and diversion risk? Which US cloud regions and chip SKUs remain available under current licence review policies? Those questions belong in product requirements documents, not only in outside counsel memos after a term sheet.

Public company adaptations already showed the pattern. Chip vendors designed China-specific products to navigate earlier thresholds; later rules chased those designs. Cloud providers segmented access. SEA startups that resell, fine-tune, or host models inherit the same segmentation whether or not they employ a Washington lobbyist. The control surface moves faster than most product roadmaps. Continuous screening beats annual policy theatre.

None of this resolves the political debate over whether the controls are wise. For firms that sit between US technology stacks and PRC manufacturing or talent networks, export controls are first-class constraints on architecture, hiring, and partnership. Southeast Asia’s optionality story survives for many software and consumer products. It thins quickly near advanced semiconductors and frontier AI compute. Founders who ignore that thinning discover it as a denied shipment, a frozen cloud quota, or a partner that disappears from the legal map overnight.
