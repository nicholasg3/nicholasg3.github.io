English founder media is not the customer

Across much of Southeast Asia, English-language startup coverage tracks capital and attention. Checkout, support tickets, and regulators often track another language entirely.

JAKARTA / SINGAPORE — Pitch decks, demo days, and regional tech headlines in Southeast Asia are frequently written in English. So are many investor updates and conference stages. In large national markets, a different language often governs the moment money changes hands: the app screen, the help chat, the terms page, and the complaint filed with a local authority.

That split is easy to miss on a single slide. English coverage can climb while local conversion stays flat. The fundraising narrative still looks regional. The revenue cohort does not.

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) supplies a tidy regional brand. Commercial language still breaks along national lines. Singapore, the Philippines, Vietnam, and Indonesia do not share one language ecology. The gap between English founder media and domestic demand shows up most sharply where the mass market is large and English is not the default of daily commerce.

Two audiences, one company

Regional startups typically face two audiences at once. One is a capital audience: funds, English-language tech press, conference circuits, and professional networks that hire and syndicate deals across borders. The other is a customer audience: people who complete a purchase, open a ticket, leave a review, or deal with a bank or regulator at home.

Those audiences sometimes share a language. In several Southeast Asian markets they do not. When they diverge, English press mentions and podcast bookings can look like traction while renewal and support metrics tell another story.

English-language regional outlets grew because capital and talent already move across borders. Tech in Asia, for example, publishes Asia tech news for a regional and international readership. That readership matters for fundraising and hiring. It is a thin sample of who buys on a consumer app outside the capital-city English bubble.

Indonesia’s numbers

Indonesia is a useful stress case for mass and mid-market products. Its digital base is large. Its constitutional national language is Indonesian, known as Bahasa Indonesia.

Article 36 of the 1945 Constitution, as amended and published by the Constitute Project, states that the national language shall be Indonesian. Consumer notices, many government interfaces, and much mass-market product copy sit inside that legal and practical frame even when a fundraising narrative is drafted in English.

Scale does not settle the language question by itself. DataReportal’s Digital 2024: Indonesia report, part of the We Are Social and Meltwater series, estimated 185.3 million internet users in early 2024, or about 66.5 percent of a population of roughly 278.7 million. The figures describe a large digital population. They do not describe how much of that population does business in English.

What English tests show

Household censuses rarely ask whether a user can finish an English checkout. Private testing firms publish relative scores instead.

According to the EF English Proficiency Index country page for Indonesia, the country ranked 80th globally with a score of 471, compared with a global average of 488 on the same page. EF Education First is a private company; the sample is people who take its tests. On that instrument, Indonesia sits below the global average and well outside the highest-proficiency group of small open economies. The index is a rough comparative signal. It still undercuts the habit of reading English founder social media as a demand gauge for Indonesia’s mass market.

English as a regional work language

English remains common in multi-country teams, contracts, and investor reporting. Applied linguists describe that pattern as English used as a lingua franca among multilingual professionals, rather than as a replacement for national languages at home.

Andy Kirkpatrick’s English as a Lingua Franca in ASEAN is a standard book-length account of that multilingual model. The frame fits a familiar scene: an English board pack beside local-language app reviews and support threads. Lingua-franca traffic among mobile professionals is a weak substitute for evidence about domestic majority demand.

Where the signals part company

Companies that track both layers often see a clean split between English narrative metrics and revenue-language operations. The table below summarizes how those signals diverge in practice.

Signal What it often tracks Where it misleads
English press and podcasts Capital and media attention Coverage rises while local checkout and renewals stay flat
Support tickets by language Actual service load English help centers meet a local-language ticket queue
Checkout completion by interface language Where payments clear English creative wins awards while local interfaces own conversion
Reviews and local social proof Where the next cohort forms English social praise with thin local review surfaces
Terms and partner documents Language of record with banks and regulators English legalese on screens while partners work in the national language

When English narrative metrics move and the operational rows do not, the company is winning attention in the capital layer. Revenue still depends on the other rows.

How the split shows up at work

Hiring often follows the English story. Growth and communications roles that write for investors fill first. Community and support capacity in the revenue language lag.

Performance teams test English creative because their tools, dashboards, and agency partners are English-first. Local competitors run the same funnels in the language of the feed and sometimes buy the same users more cheaply.

Product ships with English-first microcopy. Localization arrives after churn and ratings have already moved. Legal drafts start in English; consumer-facing notices and partner packets later need a second language under time pressure.

What operators are measuring

Teams that separate the two publics tend to keep parallel scoreboards. One tracks English coverage, inbound investor interest, and cross-border hiring. The other tracks checkout completion, ticket language, review language, and the language of contracts with banks and regulators in the wedge market.

Where those scoreboards diverge for a sustained period, some companies cut English-only acquisition spend until revenue-language conversion catches up. Others keep English spend for capital markets work and fund local-language product and support as a separate line. Both responses treat English founder media as a market for attention and capital, not as a census of demand.

Closing

English founder media in Southeast Asia is a real market. It prices attention, status, and access to capital. Demand in large non-English-majority markets still clears in the language of the checkout, the ticket, and the local partner file.

The practical story is measurement. Coverage and capital can be scored in English. Revenue still has to be scored where the money moves.
