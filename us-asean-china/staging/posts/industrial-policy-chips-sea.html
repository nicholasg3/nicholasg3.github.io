Friendshoring reallocates chip attention to SEA

U.S. industrial policy does not only build American fabs. It changes which Southeast Asian nodes get capital and diplomatic focus.

KUALA LUMPUR — Semiconductor politics used to sound like a Taiwan Strait brief for specialists. After the United States passed the CHIPS and Science Act in 2022, the same politics became a plant-siting and supplier-qualification problem for Southeast Asia. Friendshoring reallocates subsidies, export-control risk, and board attention across the global value chain. That includes nodes that assemble, test, and package chips far from leading-edge logic fabs.

Southeast Asia will not replace Taiwan or South Korea at the most advanced process nodes. It already sits inside the chain. When Washington pays firms to reshore and partners to diversify, Malaysian packaging lines, Vietnamese electronics clusters, and Singaporean specialty roles become strategic objects, not only cost centers.

Friendshoring reallocates plants; it also reallocates political attention—and SEA mid-stream nodes are on the map.

The CHIPS Act as a global shock

President Biden signed the CHIPS and Science Act on 9 August 2022. White House fact sheets and later policy analysis describe tens of billions of dollars in semiconductor manufacturing and research incentives—commonly summarized as about $52 billion for semiconductor incentives within a larger science and competitiveness package. The NIST CHIPS for America program is the domestic surface for manufacturing grants, R&D, and workforce efforts.

The Act’s primary political sale is American production and jobs. Its secondary effect is global. Firms that take U.S. money face expectations about capacity location, China expansion, and supply-chain security. CSIS analysis of semiconductor supply chains and the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework argues that reshoring via CHIPS has not automatically been matched by a coherent friendshoring trade strategy—and that aligning the two would matter for long-run resilience. For Southeast Asian firms, the practical point is simpler: U.S. industrial policy now sits on the same spreadsheet as labor cost and logistics.

Vietnam on the ITSI map

The CHIPS Act also created the International Technology Security and Innovation (ITSI) Fund for the State Department. On 11 September 2023, the Department announced a partnership with Vietnam to explore semiconductor supply-chain opportunities under ITSI. The announcement emphasizes Vietnam’s existing strengths in assembly, testing, and packaging, workforce development, and diversification of the global ecosystem. It does not claim that Hanoi will host leading-edge fabs tomorrow.

That diplomatic product is friendshoring language made concrete. It tells investors and multinationals that Washington will spend political and program capital on a Southeast Asian partner for mid-stream resilience. Vietnamese industrial policy incentives and multinational electronics investments predate ITSI. The new layer is explicit linkage to U.S. chip industrial policy.

Malaysia’s packaging weight

Malaysia has long been a major semiconductor exporter through assembly, testing, and packaging (ATP) rather than only through advanced foundry marketing. An ISIS Malaysia analysis of the national semiconductor ecosystem amid geopolitical flux states that Malaysia is the sixth-largest semiconductor exporter globally and commands about 13 percent of global ATP. Those figures place the country inside any serious diversification story even when newspaper maps only show Arizona and Taiwan.

Homeshoring in the United States and Europe does not delete ATP demand. In some scenarios it raises the value of trusted packaging and test capacity outside China. Malaysian policy debates about upgrading into higher-value segments sit next to U.S. and Japanese firm location choices. Friendshoring is more than “move a factory from Shenzhen to Penang.” It is also “which existing node gets the next toolset and the next audit for U.S. and allied customers.” That audit can matter more than a ribbon-cutting photo for whether a line stays in a trusted supplier set.

Industrial policy is a spillover machine. U.S. subsidies reshape relative returns. Allied export controls reshape customer lists. Southeast Asian governments answer with their own incentives, training programs, and national semiconductor strategies. Founders in electronics, industrial software, specialty chemicals, and logistics feel the pull even when they never apply for a CHIPS grant.

Export controls and entity lists sit next to subsidy politics. A plant that looks “China+1” on a slide can still sit inside a controlled tool chain or a customer base that must screen end uses. Friendshoring is a re-sorting of which jurisdictions and partners look acceptable to U.S. and allied buyers under stress. Boards that only track tax holidays miss that screening layer.

Singapore’s role often looks different from Malaysia’s ATP story or Vietnam’s assembly growth: headquarters, specialty manufacturing, equipment services, and finance. That specialization is still industrial-policy-adjacent. When multinationals rebalance Asia footprints, they need legal, treasury, and engineering nodes that can talk to both Washington risk teams and Asian factory managers. City-state capacity becomes part of the friendshoring plumbing even when wafer starts happen elsewhere.

The honest map is layered. Leading-edge logic remains concentrated. Advanced packaging and ATP already run through Malaysia and other regional nodes. Vietnam is a named partner in U.S. diversification programming. Singapore continues to host headquarters, specialty manufacturing, and financing roles. Friendshoring does not make ASEAN one chip market. It does make semiconductor politics a permanent input to plant and product decisions across the region.
