Dual-use founders inherit continuous export screening

For firms that touch US technology controls while operating through Southeast Asia, sanctions and export rules are product and ops constraints. A Singapore address does not put the company outside the net.

SINGAPORE — Dual-use products sit on a fault line. The same sensor, model weight, radio module, or storage controller can serve a civilian customer and a restricted end user. Founders who treat export controls as a yearly legal memo will discover the constraint in a blocked shipment, a frozen bank rail, or a multi-year enforcement action. In the US–China technology contest, Southeast Asia is not a neutral sandbox. It is a production and re-export theater where US rules still reach.

Two US regimes dominate the practical stack. The Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security administers the Export Administration Regulations and the Entity List for dual-use and certain other items. The Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control administers sanctions programs and lists of restricted persons. They are different toolkits. Both can end a customer relationship that looked ordinary on a sales dashboard.

Sanctions and export screening are continuous operations for dual-use founders — not a one-time market-entry checkbox.

The Seagate case as a SEA-relevant primary

On 19 April 2023, BIS announced a $300 million civil penalty against Seagate Technology LLC (Fremont, California) and Seagate Singapore International Headquarters Pte. Ltd. for alleged violations related to hard disk drive sales to Huawei after foreign direct product rules restricted such shipments. BIS described the resolution as the largest standalone administrative penalty in its history and said Seagate admitted the conduct in the proposed charging letter. The agency’s public release names both the US and Singapore entities.

Seagate’s own investor announcement the same day confirmed the settlement and the payment schedule. The company stated that subsidiaries Seagate Technology LLC and Seagate Singapore International Headquarters Pte. Ltd. had reached agreement with BIS over hard disk drive sales to Huawei between 17 August 2020 and 29 September 2021 that, according to BIS, did not comply with the Export Administration Regulations. The geography of the settlement is the point for regional founders. A Singapore headquarters entity was on the caption. Manufacturing and sales decisions outside the continental United States did not place the transactions beyond EAR analysis once US-controlled technology content and Huawei-specific foreign direct product rules were in play.

Industry analyses of the case emphasize that Seagate continued large Huawei volumes after competitors stopped, and that BIS viewed the foreign direct product rule as covering the drives. Exact unit counts and profit estimates vary by write-up; the load-bearing institutional facts do not. Penalty size, named Singapore entity, Huawei as restricted counterparty, and FDP theory are enough to ground the operational lesson.

What continuous screening looks like

Control surface Who runs it Where founders get surprised
Entity List / MEU List BIS Customer looks commercial; listed affiliate sits in the ownership chain
Foreign direct product rules BIS / EAR “We made it in SEA” still needs a license analysis
OFAC SDN and program sanctions Treasury OFAC Bank blocks the wire after the invoice already shipped
Ownership / affiliates tests BIS and OFAC logics 50% rules pull unnamed entities into restricted status
End-use / end-user diligence Exporter responsibility Distributor story does not match the real installation site

BIS has continued to update Entity List entries and, in 2025, expanded affiliate coverage logic so that ownership thresholds can pull non-listed entities into Entity List-style restrictions. Counsel notes of the “Affiliates Rule” stress an affirmative duty to understand ownership. For a founder selling through regional distributors, that duty is operationally heavy. It requires more than a website check. It requires ownership diligence that sales teams are not trained to perform unless compliance is built into the deal desk.

OFAC’s public materials and list search tools remain the sanctions counterpart. A clean BIS analysis does not automatically clear OFAC, and the reverse is also true. Dual-use founders who only staff one side of the stack will eventually meet the other side in a bank compliance review.

Why SEA operating models feel the squeeze

Southeast Asian hubs attract manufacturing, regional HQ functions, and re-export trade. Those are features of the business climate. They are also the fact patterns that create diversion risk in US enforcement narratives: goods or software with US content move through a third country toward a restricted party. The Seagate settlement is a large-corporate illustration. Smaller dual-use startups face the same legal theories with thinner compliance budgets.

Product design choices become control choices. A feature that enables remote sensing, encryption, autonomous navigation, or high-performance compute can change classification and licensing. Customer verticals that look attractive — ports, mining, smart cities, logistics — can sit next to government and military end users. A commercial-only SKU without end-use contractual teeth is a hope, not a control.

Ops, not ornament

Dual-use and US-touch firms working through Southeast Asia need continuous screening of customers, owners, end uses, and product classification. Singapore and other regional addresses do not create a safe harbor from EAR foreign direct product analysis. The Seagate–Huawei penalty is public proof that US and Singapore entities can share a caption when BIS believes the rules were ignored.

Boards that still file this under “legal will review annually” are mis-classifying a product constraint. Screening belongs next to billing and support as a recurring system: ownership diligence, denied-party checks, license determinations, and the power to stop a shipment when the story does not clear. That system is expensive. The alternative, priced by BIS in one case at three hundred million dollars, is more expensive.
