Port competition is a services stack fight

Strait hubs re-allocate containers and the bunkering, registry, and trade services that ride on them. Singapore still leads on TEUs. Peers are closing ranks.

SINGAPORE / PORT KLANG — Container rankings look like sports tables. They are capital-allocation maps. When boxes shift between Singapore, Port Klang, and Tanjung Pelepas, they take schedule density, feeder patterns, and a stack of maritime services with them. Bunkering, ship registry, ship finance, digital trade platforms, and headquarters staff do not sit in a separate universe from TEU counts. They follow the same coast.

Southeast Asia’s main hubs all printed strong volumes in recent years as Chinese export routes and Red Sea disruptions reshuffled east–west strings. Absolute leadership and relative growth are different questions. Singapore remains the region’s container giant. Malaysian ports have used capacity investment and pricing to climb global lists. The competitive story is not “Singapore is finished.” It is that peers are real, measurable, and building for the next decade of relay cargo.

Port competition reallocates trade routes and the services stacked on them—not only the box count on a ranking poster.

Singapore’s scale and stack

According to The Business Times’ 13 January 2026 report on Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore (MPA) data, Singapore’s 2025 box throughput rose 8.6 percent to about 44.7 million TEUs, with vessel arrival gross tonnage near 3.2 billion (+3.5 percent). The same report said those volumes should keep Singapore as the world’s second-busiest container port behind Shanghai, ahead of Ningbo-Zhoushan, matching the 2024 ordering in World Shipping Council-style rankings. Marine fuel sales rose to about 56.8 million tonnes, including two million tonnes of alternative marine fuels. The Singapore Registry of Ships moved to fourth-largest globally by gross tonnage in the report’s account, helped by reflagging dynamics around U.S. fee measures on Chinese-linked ships.

Those numbers are not only cargo bragging rights. Bunker sales, registry scale, and the presence of more than 200 international shipping groups (with 35 openings or expansions in 2025 cited by the minister of state for law and transport in the same article) describe a services stack. Digital bunkering roll-out, methanol bunker licences, and LNG standards work are attempts to keep high-value activity attached to the box franchise. Throughput without the stack is a different business than throughput with it.

Malaysian peers on the board

Malaysia’s Ministry of Transport made the peer claim explicit. In a 1 September 2025 media release, MOT said Lloyd’s List ranked Port Klang 10th among the world’s busiest container ports on 2024 performance—the first time in the top 10. Throughput was 14.64 million TEUs in 2024, up 4.1 percent from 14.06 million in 2023. The ministry’s ranking path for Port Klang: 13th in 2022, 11th in 2023, 10th for the 2024/2025 list cycle. The release’s ranking table shows Port Klang moving past Hong Kong into 10th for 2024 volumes.

Port of Tanjung Pelepas (PTP) is the other Malaysian pillar. MOT reported PTP at about 12.25 million TEUs in 2024, a 16.9 percent rise from 10.48 million in 2023, holding roughly 15th place globally in both years. Together, the ministry said, Port Klang and PTP supported Malaysia’s position as the fifth-largest country by container throughput in 2024. Capacity plans are part of the competitive signal: Westports’ CT10–CT17 expansion is described as doubling capacity from 14 million to 28 million TEUs by 2028; Carey Island is framed as a long-horizon project with potential toward 30 million TEUs annual capacity by 2060.

Relative growth can favor the peer even when the absolute leader is far ahead. The Business Times quoted Linerlytica analyst Tan Hua Joo noting that main Southeast Asian hub ports hit record volumes in 2025 on strong Chinese exports outside the U.S., and that Singapore’s 8.6 percent box growth sat below Tanjung Pelepas’ 14.5 percent and above Port Klang’s 3.4 percent in that comparison. Growth rates are not destiny. They are evidence that schedule managers have real alternatives along the Strait of Malacca. Industry tables reproduced by Malaysia’s ministry, drawing on Lloyd’s List ranking data, are how that peer status becomes a public industrial-policy fact rather than a private operator claim.

For founders and operators in logistics software, trade finance, and maritime services, the practical read is not “pick a winner.” It is that product demand follows where boxes and ships actually call. A customs API, a bunker-procurement tool, or a trade-document workflow that only models Singapore will miss the Malaysian share of relay and gateway cargo. A regional go-to-market that assumes one hub forever will mis-price sales coverage along the Strait.

States already treat the contest as industrial policy. Singapore invests in multi-fuel bunkering standards and digital process efficiency. The Business Times report notes MPA’s digital bunkering roll-out, methanol bunker licence awards, and work on LNG and ammonia technical references—investments aimed at keeping high-value energy and compliance activity tied to the port. Malaysia invests in berths, Westports expansion, and national ranking narratives. Both are rational. Entrepôt geography is path dependent, but it is not frozen. Carrier alliances rebalance strings when terminal productivity, landside costs, and feeder networks shift. The ranking table is a lagging indicator of those choices.

Johor’s adjacency to Singapore adds another layer. PTP’s growth is not only “Malaysia versus Singapore” in abstract. It is a Strait system in which some cargo optimizes for land bridges, free-zone manufacturing, and dual-gateway options. Shippers that can choose among PSA, Port Klang, and PTP treat reliability and total landed cost as a portfolio, not a loyalty brand.

Singapore’s lead in absolute TEUs remains large. The peer story is still the story that matters for the next round of capital expenditure: Port Klang in the global top ten, PTP growing double digits in a documented year, and a services stack fight that will not be settled by a single annual TEU print.
