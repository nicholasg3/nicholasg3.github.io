English founder media is not the customer

English-language startup coverage in Southeast Asia measures attention and capital fairly well. As a gauge of who actually pays at checkout, it is thin.

By Nicholas Garcia — Lecturer, NUS School of Computing · Deputy Director, NUS FinTech Lab

SINGAPORE — A regional startup can be loud in English and quiet where its money clears. Pitch decks, demo days, investor updates and most tech headlines are written in English. In the large national markets, though, the payment screen, the support chat and the bank partner file often run in another language.

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) gives the region one label, but commerce splits by country, and Singapore, the Philippines, Vietnam and Indonesia do not share one language ecology. English coverage can therefore rise while local conversion stays flat. A company can raise money on a regional story while its paying customers sit mostly in one country and one language.

The public that funds a regional company and the public that pays it often read in different languages, and the gap between them is easy to mistake for traction.

Two audiences

Most regional companies sell to two audiences at once. The capital audience is made up of funds, the English-language tech press, conference circuits and cross-border hiring networks. The customer audience is shoppers, people writing support tickets, reviewers, banks and regulators, and it frequently works in a different language from the first group.

When the two diverge, podcast bookings and press mentions can look like traction while renewals and support load say something else. English regional media grew because capital and talent already crossed borders. Tech in Asia, which covers Asian tech for a regional and international readership, is one example. Its readers matter for fundraising and hiring, but they are a weak sample of who buys a consumer app outside the capital-city English bubble.

GrabChat, then Grab and Uber

On 25 October 2016, ABS-CBN reported that Grab had launched GrabChat, an in-app messaging tool for drivers and passengers. The company history summarized on the Grab Holdings encyclopedia entry, which cites that report among other sources, says the feature handled simple communication and translated messages when driver and passenger language settings differed. The problem it solved was concrete: two people on the same trip might not share a language, and the company shipped software to deal with that.

By then English founder media had been covering Southeast Asian ride-hailing as one regional story for years. GrabChat was a small feature, but it showed where the business actually ran. Whatever language the press wrote in, a trip still failed if the driver and rider could not agree on a pickup.

In March 2018, Grab acquired Uber’s Southeast Asia operations. Grab’s press release called the deal the largest of its kind in the region and said transport would expand with “more localised transport services,” alongside food delivery and payments. Uber’s then-CEO, Dara Khosrowshahi, was quoted looking ahead to Grab’s future in Southeast Asia. BBC News reported the same day that Uber was selling its South East Asia business to Grab.

Capital, regulation and product breadth all shaped that outcome, and language was one thread in a larger localization story. English coverage of both brands was abundant. Yet after the deal, Grab’s own framing leaned on local transport modes, payments and operating fit, which is the part of the business English regional coverage tends to describe least.

Indonesia makes the split hard to ignore

Article 36 of the 1945 Constitution, as amended and published by the Constitute Project, names Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) as the national language. For mass and mid-market products, that means consumer notices, many government interfaces and much of the product copy are in Indonesian even when the fundraising story is drafted in English.

The digital base is large. DataReportal’s Digital 2024: Indonesia report, from the We Are Social and Meltwater series, estimated 185.3 million internet users in early 2024, about 66.5 percent of a population near 278.7 million. Those figures describe scale; they do not show how much of that base does business in English.

Household surveys rarely ask whether someone can finish an English checkout, so private testers fill the gap with relative scores. The EF English Proficiency Index page for Indonesia ranked the country 80th globally with a score of 471, against a global average of 488 on the same page. EF Education First is a private firm and its sample is people who take its tests, so the index is only a rough comparative signal. On it, Indonesia sits below the global average and well outside the highest-proficiency group of small open economies. Even a rough signal like that makes English founder social media a weak gauge of demand in Indonesia’s mass market.

English still does real work in multi-country teams, contracts and investor reporting, a pattern applied linguists call English as a lingua franca among multilingual professionals. Andy Kirkpatrick’s English as a Lingua Franca in ASEAN is the standard book-length account. The frame fits Grab as well as Indonesia, because English regional coverage and board packs can run alongside driver–rider translation and national-language consumer screens, and the English traffic among mobile professionals says little about demand from the domestic majority.

Measuring English attention and local revenue separately

Companies that track both layers usually find that English narrative metrics and revenue-language operations move on different clocks.

Signal What it often tracks Where it misleads
English press and podcasts Capital and media attention Coverage rises while local checkout and renewals stay flat
Support tickets by language Actual service load English help centers meet a local-language ticket queue
Checkout completion by interface language Where payments clear English creative wins awards while local interfaces own conversion
Reviews and local social proof Where the next cohort forms English social praise with thin local review surfaces
Terms and partner documents Language of record with banks and regulators English legalese on screens while partners work in the national language

If the English metrics rise while the operational rows stay flat, the company is gaining attention among investors and the press, and revenue still depends on the rows that have not moved. The split shows up in ordinary hiring and spending decisions. Investor-facing roles get filled first, while support capacity in the revenue language lags. Performance teams test English creative because their tools and agencies are English-first. Localization arrives after churn has already moved, and legal drafts start in English, so consumer notices and partner documents get produced in a hurry later.

Some teams keep two sets of numbers on purpose. One follows English coverage, inbound investor interest and cross-border hiring. The other follows checkout completion, the language of tickets and reviews, and the language of bank and regulator documents in the first market. When the two series diverge for months, English-only acquisition spend often gets cut. Alternatively, English spend is kept for capital markets and local-language product and support become a separate budget line. In both cases the company treats English founder media as a market for attention and capital, and measures demand on the checkout and support numbers.

English founder media in Southeast Asia is a real market, and it prices status, access and fundraising velocity. Revenue, though, has to be counted in whichever language ships the product: in the large markets where English is not the majority language, customers pay in the language of the checkout, the support ticket and the local partner file.

Reader Decision Which public is the growth review scoring?

Market Signal English founder media prices capital, not mass demand.

Regional English tech coverage and conference circuits move attention and fundraising access. Mass-market demand in places like Indonesia still clears largely in the national language.
